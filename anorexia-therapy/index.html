Anorexia Therapy in Charlotte, NC

Restriction was never really about food. It was about control, safety, and managing a world that felt like too much. There’s another way to find that safety, and I can help you get there.

Restriction Was Trying to Protect You

Long before the food rules, you learned that needing less made you easier to be around. Restriction is that lesson taken to its logical end. It is self-abandonment with a discipline that looks, from the outside, like strength.

Anorexia is one of the most misunderstood struggles a person can carry, often mistaken for vanity, willpower, or a phase that will pass on its own. It’s none of those things. Restriction is almost always a nervous system strategy: a way of creating a sense of control and safety when everything else feels unpredictable.

Whether you have a formal anorexia diagnosis, an atypical anorexia experience, or simply find yourself rigidly controlling food in ways that are quietly taking over your life, you don’t have to keep managing this alone, and you don’t have to wait until things feel dire enough to deserve support.

I’m Alynne Davis, LCMHC, an eating disorder therapist serving women in Charlotte, NC and via telehealth across North Carolina and South Carolina. I work with women who are ready to understand what restriction has been doing for them, and to find other ways to feel safe in their bodies and their lives.

Who This Is For

You might be looking for an anorexia therapist in Charlotte if any of this sounds familiar:

  • Food rules and restriction have become rigid, and deviating from them feels frightening
  • You feel a sense of control or calm when you restrict, and panic or shame when you can’t
  • Your thoughts about food, weight, or body shape take up a significant part of your day
  • You’ve been told you don’t “look sick enough” to have a real problem, even though something feels deeply wrong
  • You’re medically stable and looking for ongoing outpatient support, whether starting fresh or continuing care after a treatment program
  • Restriction feels tied to perfectionism, anxiety, trauma, or a need to hold everything together
  • You’re exhausted by a body and mind that are constantly at war with each other

If you recognize yourself here, you’re not vain or dramatic or seeking attention. You’re someone whose nervous system found a strategy for safety that has stopped serving you. That strategy can be understood, and it can change.

What Anorexia Therapy Actually Looks Like

We don’t start by trying to talk you out of your fear. We start with curiosity: what is restriction protecting you from? What does safety actually feel like in your body? What would it mean to trust yourself with food again?

Understanding What Restriction Is Doing For You

Before anything can change, we need to understand why restriction developed in the first place, not as a flaw, but as a strategy that made sense at some point. This isn’t about judgment. It’s about getting curious enough to work with the pattern instead of against it.

Nervous System Regulation

Restriction often becomes the nervous system’s way of managing anxiety, overwhelm, or a felt sense of danger. The work here is building real regulation capacity, other, more sustainable ways to feel safe and steady, so restriction isn’t the only tool available. Read more about nervous system dysregulation and how it connects to eating patterns.

Working With Control and Safety

For many women, restriction is fundamentally about control, feeling like at least one thing can be managed when everything else feels uncertain. We explore where that need for control comes from and build other, more flexible ways to meet it.

Body Image and Identity Beyond Food

Anorexia often becomes tangled up with identity, a sense of achievement, discipline, or worth attached to restriction. Untangling that takes time and care. We work toward a relationship with your body and your worth that isn’t contingent on shrinking.

Trauma Work

For many women, restriction is tied to unprocessed experiences, perfectionism, control that was taken away in other areas of life, or a body that learned early it wasn’t safe to take up space. When that’s part of the picture, we work with it directly. Explore the connection between trauma and eating disorders.

Working Safely, Together With Your Medical Team

Anorexia recovery is safest with a full team in place. Outpatient therapy addresses the emotional and relational roots of restriction, it works alongside your physician and a dietitian, not in place of them, so that your medical stability and nutritional needs are being monitored by the right people. If you don’t already have a doctor or dietitian, I can help you find ones who work well with therapy clients.

Outpatient counseling is appropriate for women who are medically stable and ready for ongoing, weekly support, whether you’re just beginning to address restriction or continuing care after a higher level of care. If, at any point, your safety calls for more support than weekly outpatient therapy can provide, an intensive outpatient program, residential, or inpatient care, I’ll help you find the right referral rather than trying to stretch outpatient therapy past where it’s safe. We can talk through what level of care makes sense for you on a free consultation.

Therapy Approaches I Use

Expressive Arts Therapy

Restriction often carries feelings and experiences that are hard to put into words, fear, shame, a need for control that runs deeper than logic. Expressive arts therapy gives that experience another way to move, through writing, visual art, imagery, and creative process, reaching what talk alone sometimes can’t.

Somatic and Body-Based Approaches

Anorexia often involves real disconnection from the body, from hunger, fullness, and physical sensation more broadly. Somatic approaches help you slow down and rebuild a felt sense of safety in your body, one that’s based on curiosity and trust rather than control and management.

IFS, Internal Family Systems

IFS helps us understand the part that restricts, not as the enemy, but as a protector trying to keep you safe in the only way it knows how. When we can meet that part with compassion instead of a fight, real change becomes possible.

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT)

CBT helps identify the thoughts and beliefs that keep restriction in place, the all-or-nothing rules, the fear-based predictions, the inner voice equating control with safety. We work to loosen those patterns and build more flexible, compassionate ways of relating to food and your body.

What Working Together Looks Like

Your First Session

Your first session is a space to share your story, where things stand right now, what you’ve tried before, and what you’re hoping for. There’s no checklist to complete. Just a conversation about where you are and where you want to go, including an honest read on whether outpatient therapy is the right level of care for you.

Weekly Sessions

Sessions are 50 minutes, typically weekly. We move at a pace that feels safe and honest, steady enough to hold the real work, without moving faster than feels safe for your nervous system. The goal isn’t perfection. It’s a genuinely different relationship with yourself and with food.

In Person & Telehealth

I practice in Charlotte, NC and offer telehealth to clients throughout North Carolina and South Carolina, including Raleigh, Asheville, Durham, Wilmington, Columbia SC, and Greenville SC. Many women find that telehealth creates more ease for this kind of work, you’re in your own space, with no commute after a session that went somewhere real.

Free 15-Minute Consultation

A free consultation is available before you commit to anything. It’s a chance to ask questions, share a bit about where you are, and talk honestly about whether outpatient therapy, and working with me specifically, is the right fit. No pressure, no forms.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is anorexia and how is it treated in therapy?

Anorexia involves restricting food intake, often alongside an intense fear of weight gain and a distorted relationship with body image, but underneath the behavior is almost always a story about control, safety, and managing a world that feels overwhelming. Outpatient therapy addresses the emotional and nervous system roots of restriction, not just the eating behavior, through trauma-informed, weight-inclusive care. At Alynne Davis, PLLC, treatment includes nervous system regulation, exploring what restriction has been protecting against, and rebuilding a trusting relationship with food and the body.

Is anorexia therapy available in Charlotte, NC?

Yes. Alynne Davis, LCMHC offers anorexia therapy for women in Charlotte, NC and via telehealth across North Carolina and South Carolina. Sessions are available in-person and online for clients who are medically stable and appropriate for outpatient care.

Do you provide medical monitoring or nutrition counseling for anorexia?

No, outpatient therapy addresses the emotional, relational, and trauma-based roots of anorexia, and works alongside a client’s medical provider and dietitian rather than replacing them. Anorexia recovery is safest with a full team in place. If you don’t already have a physician or dietitian, I can help you find one as part of getting started.

What if I need a higher level of care than outpatient therapy?

Anorexia can require different levels of care depending on medical stability, from outpatient counseling to intensive outpatient programs to residential or inpatient treatment. A free consultation is a chance to talk through where you are right now. If a higher level of care would serve you better than weekly outpatient therapy, I’ll help you find the right referral rather than trying to fit you into a level of care that isn’t safe.

How is anorexia therapy different from treatment for other eating disorders?

Anorexia often centers on control and a fear response that food and weight gain feel unsafe, which shapes how therapy unfolds, with particular attention to safety, medical collaboration, and the pace of change. The underlying work, understanding what the behavior has been protecting you from, nervous system regulation, and rebuilding self-trust, draws on the same trauma-informed, expressive arts-based approach used across eating disorder treatment at Alynne Davis, PLLC, adapted to what restriction specifically requires.

Alynne Davis, LCMHC offers anorexia therapy in person in Charlotte, NC and via telehealth throughout North Carolina and South Carolina, including Raleigh, Asheville, Durham, Wilmington, Columbia SC, and Greenville SC.
