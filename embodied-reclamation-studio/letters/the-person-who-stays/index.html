Letters from the Studio, Letter Three

The Person Who Stays

What it really means to return to yourself. If remembering is the beginning of healing, then return is the quiet practice of staying with ourselves, especially in the moments we most want to leave.

Lately I’ve been wondering if healing isn’t really about changing ourselves at all.

Maybe it’s about learning to stay with ourselves.

Until you’re sitting with disappointment.

Or loneliness.

Or shame.

Or the ache of wanting someone to choose you.

That’s when most of us quietly leave.

I remember one morning when I reached the end of myself.

Life had been asking more of me than I felt I had to give. I was carrying the quiet weight of parenting, running a practice, holding together a home, worrying about my son’s mental health, and trying to keep going on an exhausted body that had very little left to offer. That morning, one more missed alarm became the tipping point.

I snapped.

I yelled so loudly I’m sure the neighbors could have heard me.

And almost as quickly as the words left my mouth, a wave of guilt crashed over me. It landed like a punch to the stomach. My heart raced so fiercely I could feel it beating in my throat.

Becoming the Person I Needed

I wanted to collapse into someone’s arms, but I had no one else to collapse into but myself.

As I drove home, I turned off the music. The silence felt almost louder than my own thoughts. My breathing became shallow, almost like the beginning of a panic attack. Tears welled up like hot springs behind my eyes before spilling down my cheeks.

Then the familiar voices arrived.

You’re a terrible mother.

You should know better.

You’re a therapist, for goodness’ sake.

But underneath those voices was another one. Much quieter.

You’re exhausted.

You’re carrying more than anyone can see.

Without even thinking, I pressed the palm of my hand against my sternum, almost as if my own heart needed someone to hold it.

My first instinct was to reach for someone else.

I wanted someone to tell me I wasn’t a terrible person.

I wanted someone to make the guilt disappear.

Instead, I reached for my phone and pressed record, not to explain myself to someone else, but to tell myself the truth.

“This hurts.”

“I’m overwhelmed.”

“I’m grieving.”

“I’m trying.”

Looking back, I don’t think the extraordinary thing was that I calmed down.

The extraordinary thing was that I stayed.

For perhaps the first time…

I didn’t leave.

I became the kind of presence I had spent a lifetime hoping to find.

When I got home, I didn’t rush to fix what had happened.

I took a walk because movement has become one of the ways I remind my body that it is safe.

I stood beneath the hot water of the shower a little longer than usual.

Later, I wrapped my hands around a warm mug of hibiscus tea. As the steam rose toward my face and the deep crimson color slowly unfurled in the cup, I noticed something I hadn’t expected.

My breathing had slowed.

Somewhere between the walk, the shower, and the quiet ritual of making tea, my body had begun believing it was safe again.

I went to work that day, not because I had pushed my feelings aside, but because I had made space for them first. I could be fully present with my clients because I had first chosen to be present with myself.

Later that evening, I realized I had practiced the very thing I spend so much time inviting my clients to practice.

I had stayed with myself.

The Many Ways We Leave Ourselves

That’s where self-presence lives.

Until that day, I hadn’t realized how many different ways I had learned to leave myself.

My instinct wasn’t simply to feel better.

My instinct was to escape feeling.

Sometimes through productivity.

Sometimes through another person’s reassurance.

Sometimes through romance.

Sometimes through distraction.

Sometimes through fixing myself.

Sometimes through numbing.

The strategy changed.

The intention stayed the same.

Leave before the feeling gets too close.

I wasn’t trying to escape the emotion.

I was trying to escape being alone with it.

Not because any of those things are wrong.

But because somewhere along the way I had learned that difficult emotions were places I wasn’t supposed to stay very long.

Return Is a Relationship

Self-abandonment doesn’t always look like leaving ourselves.

Sometimes it looks like fixing ourselves.

Sometimes we mistake self-improvement for self-relationship.

We become experts at understanding ourselves…

while quietly avoiding being with ourselves.

…instead of simply staying.

And perhaps that’s what return really is.

Not solving.

Staying.

Presence isn’t simply paying attention.

Self-presence isn’t simply noticing what’s happening inside us. It’s choosing not to abandon ourselves while it’s happening.

It is becoming a safe place to land when life feels anything but safe.

It is discovering that the relationship you have with yourself may be the most important relationship you will ever cultivate.

It is learning that our emotions are not emergencies.

They are experiences asking for our presence.

Healing isn’t measured by whether we have difficult mornings.

It’s measured by whether we abandon ourselves when we do.

Learning to Stay

We spend much of childhood hoping someone else will stay with us.

Healing begins when we become the person who finally does.

Every time we stay with ourselves instead of abandoning ourselves…

we are returning.

Return isn’t the absence of pain.

Return is the decision that no emotion,

no mistake,

no shame,

no disappointment,

no heartbreak will convince us to leave ourselves again.

Maybe that is what embodiment has been asking of us all along.

Not perfection.

Presence.

Not becoming someone different.

Simply becoming the person who stays.

Until next time, may you remember who you are, return to yourself with compassion, and reclaim the life that has been waiting for you.

This week’s practice

Remember

Healing isn’t measured by whether you have difficult moments.

It’s measured by whether you abandon yourself when you do.

Return

The next time you notice yourself feeling overwhelmed, ashamed, or anxious, pause before trying to fix the feeling.

Place one hand over your heart or wherever your body most needs your attention.

Take a slow breath and gently ask:

“What would it look like to stay with myself right now?”

Notice whatever arises without trying to change it. Just practice staying.

Reclaim

This week, notice one moment when your instinct is to leave yourself.

Maybe you reach for your phone.

Maybe you start criticizing yourself.

Maybe you distract, explain, or try to fix what you’re feeling.

Instead, pause for just one minute.

Offer yourself the same presence you would offer someone you deeply love.

Then ask yourself:

“What changes when I become the person who stays?”
