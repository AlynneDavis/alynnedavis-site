Letters from the Studio

When Being Human Feels Like Failure

What happens when the qualities we once relied on to create a sense of identity become the very standards we use to judge and criticize ourselves?

Perhaps being organized helped us feel steady. Planning helped us feel prepared. Productivity helped us feel useful. Being dependable helped us feel safe in our relationships.

Somewhere along the way, dependability may have become more than a quality. It became a quiet agreement we made with ourselves:

If I follow through, no one will be disappointed.

If no one is disappointed, I still belong.

We may never have spoken these words aloud, but our bodies learned them. Saying yes became a way to protect connection. Following through became proof that we were still trustworthy, wanted, and worthy of belonging.

These qualities may have begun as survival adaptations, wise and protective responses that helped us create a sense of control when everything around us felt painfully out of control.

The problem is not that these parts of us existed. They may have carried us through experiences we did not yet have the language, support, or safety to process.

The problem begins when we mistake these protective strategies for the entirety of who we are.

We begin to believe that being organized is who we are, rather than something we are sometimes able to do. We believe that being productive proves our worth. We trust the version of ourselves who can manage, plan, anticipate, and follow through, while judging the version who becomes tired, uncertain, overwhelmed, or in need of rest.

What once helped us feel safe can slowly become the standard we use to harm ourselves.

The deeper truth is that we have often mistaken identity for capacity.

Capacity changes. It moves with grief, stress, trauma, hormones, illness, caregiving, relationships, seasons, and the ordinary demands of being alive.

A change in capacity does not mean we have lost our identity.

It means we are human.

And being human does not erase who we are.

It may actually bring us closer to the parts of ourselves that productivity, perfectionism, and constant competence have kept hidden.

There is something beautiful and fragile about being human. Yet we live in a culture that often frowns upon the most human parts of us: needing rest, changing our minds, becoming overwhelmed, moving slowly, asking for help, or not knowing what we will be able to offer tomorrow.

Perhaps the failure is not that we cannot always meet the standards we once created.

Perhaps the failure is believing we were ever supposed to be limitless.

When the Calendar Becomes a Cage

This is why something as simple as a date on the calendar can begin to feel so heavy.

There may have been a time when making plans felt natural, maybe even life-giving. A full social calendar meant connection, possibility, and something to look forward to.

But somewhere along the way, the calendar that once represented freedom can begin to feel like a cage.

We look at an invitation waiting for a response and wonder:

What if I am too tired by the time Friday actually arrives?

What if I do not have the energy to get ready?

What will people think if I change my mind?

Who will I disappoint if I realize that what I need most is rest?

The invitation is no longer simply an invitation. It becomes a test of whether we can still be the person we believe ourselves to be.

Reliable.

Energetic.

Social.

Capable.

The person who shows up.

Beneath all of this may be an even quieter belief:

If I follow through, no one will be disappointed.

If no one is disappointed, I still belong.

But what if you have not actually lost yourself?

What if you have simply lost the capacity to keep performing a version of yourself built around constant competence?

What if your truest self could no longer remain buried beneath everything you had learned to manage?

The Life I Organized Around Survival

I remember living inside this tension vividly after leaving my last marriage.

I felt obligated to everything: my children, my family, my job, my friends, and, Lord knows, my healing.

Even healing had somehow become another responsibility I needed to manage well.

For years, I had measured myself by my ability to be the responsible one. I could organize, anticipate, make plans, and juggle what felt like a million things at once.

From the outside, these qualities may have looked like strengths.

And they were strengths.

But they were also how I survived.

I was surviving the pain and instability of living with an alcoholic husband who barely knew how to survive himself.

Being organized helped me feel sane. Planning helped me believe I could create some predictability inside a life that felt anything but predictable.

At the time, I thought the structure was giving me peace and safety.

Looking back, it felt more like a chain-link fence I had constructed around my life, the tall kind, with barbed wire wrapped across the top.

It kept chaos from spilling in, or at least gave me the illusion that it could.

But it also made it nearly impossible for me to get out.

I had spent eleven years in that relationship, and eight years before it in another abusive relationship with someone struggling with alcoholism.

For nearly two decades, I organized my life around surviving other people’s instability.

The calendar invitations, graduate school schedule, work responsibilities, and demands of being a mother and wife gave me roles to inhabit.

They told me what to do next.

They gave me a place to direct my attention so I would not have to feel the full weight of what was happening inside me.

When those roles began to change, I did not know who I was without them.

Without the schedule, who was I?

Without someone needing me, what was I supposed to do?

Without a crisis to organize myself around, what did I actually want?

I had spent so long becoming the person who could handle everything that I had very little practice being the person who could need something.

When Safety Feels Unfamiliar

That realization did not feel liberating at first.

It felt disorienting.

I had believed freedom would feel like finally having fewer demands.

Instead, freedom often felt like standing in an open field after living behind a fence for years. There was space everywhere, but I did not yet know where to go.

Perhaps this is one of the quiet truths of healing: when survival has shaped our identity, safety can initially feel unfamiliar.

Rest may feel irresponsible.

An open calendar may feel unsettling.

Changing our minds may feel like betrayal.

Saying no may feel more dangerous than continuing to abandon ourselves.

The protective parts of us may interpret flexibility as failure because rigidity once helped us survive. They may tighten their grip, insisting that we keep planning, producing, anticipating, and proving ourselves.

Not because they are trying to hurt us.

Because they remember what happened when life felt out of control.

These parts deserve our compassion.

But compassion does not mean allowing them to continue running our lives.

Making Plans Without Abandoning Ourselves

At some point, we may need to ask a different question.

What would it be like to make plans without abandoning ourselves?

What would it mean to let a plan be a possibility rather than a moral obligation?

Could rest become information rather than evidence of failure?

Could changing our minds be an act of honesty rather than a character flaw?

Embodiment asks us to notice the difference between the yes that rises from desire and the yes that comes from fear.

One feels spacious, even when it asks something of us.

The other often arrives with tightening.

A heaviness in the chest.

A knot in the stomach.

A mind already rehearsing how to endure something we have not even agreed to yet.

For many of us, not saying yes when we mean no is not a simple communication skill.

It is a reclamation.

It is learning that disappointing someone does not mean we have done something wrong.

It is learning that our needs do not become less legitimate simply because someone else may not understand them.

It is learning that we are allowed to consider the person we will be on Friday, not only the person who wants to be agreeable today.

Perhaps embodied authenticity is not about finally becoming a version of ourselves who always knows what she needs.

Perhaps it is about becoming someone who is willing to listen when her needs change.

Staying With Myself

Today, when an invitation arrives, and I notice the tightening in my chest or the clenching in my jaw, I pause.

I breathe.

I remind myself that I have choice and agency in my life.

I do not have to say yes right away.

I do not have to respond immediately.

I can sleep on it.

I can let the invitation breathe before deciding what belongs on my calendar.

And even when I say yes, I am allowed to change my mind if, when the day arrives, the commitment no longer feels aligned with what I need.

I no longer want to abandon myself in order to satisfy the status quo or protect someone else from disappointment.

But this reclamation is an ongoing practice.

The part of me that learned to manage everything is still here.

I know her well.

She has been with me for a long time, trying to protect me from the disappointment that so often led to shame.

I still hear the echoes of the old stories that once kept me overbooked and spread so thin that I could barely be present for the very things I had desperately wanted to be part of.

I did not want to miss anything.

More than that, I did not want anyone else to feel let down by me.

For a long time, I believed that following through protected my belonging.

If no one was disappointed in me, perhaps I would still be safe, still loved, and still welcome.

So I said yes when I was tired.

I stayed when I wanted to leave.

I filled the calendar until there was almost no room left for me.

Now, I am learning that disappointing someone is not the same as harming them.

Changing my mind is not the same as being unreliable.

Resting is not the same as failing.

And making space for my own needs does not mean I care less about the people I love.

It means I am learning to include myself in the life I am creating.

Perhaps this is what reclamation looks like: not silencing the old protective parts, but listening to them with compassion while choosing differently.

Not becoming someone who never feels pressure, guilt, or fear.

Becoming someone who can feel those things and still remain with herself.

With Eternal Gratitude,
Alynne

This week’s practice

As you continue your journey of remembering, returning, and reclaiming, I leave you with this week’s practice.

Remember

Your changing capacity is not evidence that you have lost yourself.

You are not only the organized one, the dependable one, or the person who always follows through.

You are also the person who needs rest, changes her mind, asks for space, and listens when her body says, not today.

Your belonging was never meant to depend on how little disappointment you caused.

Return

Bring one invitation, commitment, or unfinished decision to mind.

Before deciding what you should do, pause. Notice what happens in your body.

Does your chest tighten?

Does your jaw clench?

Does your breathing become shallow?

Place a hand wherever you notice the most sensation and ask:

Is this a yes born from desire, or a yes born from fear?

You do not have to answer immediately. Let the question breathe.

Reclaim

This week, practice giving yourself time before responding. You might say:

“Thank you for thinking of me. Let me check in with myself before I commit.”

Or:

“I would love to, but I need to leave room to see what my capacity is when the day arrives.”

Reclaiming yourself may not always look dramatic.

Sometimes it looks like waiting before saying yes.

Sometimes it looks like changing your mind.

Sometimes it looks like allowing someone to feel disappointed while you choose not to abandon yourself.

And perhaps that is one way we remember that being human is not something we have to earn the right to be.
