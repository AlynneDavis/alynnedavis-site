Letters from the Studio, Letter Six

When the Mask Becomes a Cage

On survival, intimacy, and learning to love without abandoning yourself.

There are versions of ourselves we learn to become because they keep us safe.

The capable one.

The easygoing one.

The one who knows the right thing to say.

The one who apologizes quickly but never quite lets anyone close enough to see what is underneath the apology.

The one who can explain her patterns beautifully.

The one who appears vulnerable while still carefully controlling what anyone is allowed to see.

Sometimes we call this maturity.

Sometimes we call it being high-functioning.

Sometimes we call it self-awareness.

And sometimes, if we become curious enough, we begin to recognize that it is also a performance.

Not because we are dishonest.

Because somewhere along the way, being perceived a certain way became safer than simply being.

Maybe being pleasant protected you from conflict.

Maybe achievement earned you approval.

Maybe being needed made you feel secure.

Maybe having the “right” emotional response kept everyone else comfortable.

Maybe anger, jealousy, hurt, need, confusion, or being wrong felt far too dangerous.

So you adapted.

You learned to read the room.

You learned to soften your edges.

You learned to anticipate what other people needed before they asked.

You learned how to offer the version of yourself most likely to be accepted.

And for a while, it worked.

That is the thing about survival strategies.

They usually work.

Until the thing that once protected us begins to imprison us.

When the Mask Becomes a Way of Life

We can become so practiced at performing a version of ourselves that eventually we stop recognizing the performance.

We lose contact with the quieter voice underneath it, the one that knows what we feel before we explain it away, the one that recognizes exhaustion before we call it strength, the one that whispers this doesn’t feel right while the mind is busy constructing reasons why it should.

It is almost like becoming a method actor who studies a role so intensely that eventually it becomes difficult to distinguish the character from the person underneath.

The strong one.

The protector.

The independent one.

The wounded one.

The one who is always misunderstood.

The one who never really needs anyone.

We can protect these identities fiercely because, however painful they are, they are familiar.

And familiar suffering can sometimes feel safer than unfamiliar freedom.

I learned to wear many masks throughout my life.

The mask that said I’m fine was probably both the most adaptive and the most costly. I learned early that vulnerability could be met with shame, scrutiny, or criticism, so hiding what I felt seemed safer than allowing someone to know that I actually had a tender heart.

That armor helped me survive.

Other people even saw it as strength.

Eventually, though, strength became exhaustion. It took a toll on my body, my relationships, and my sense of self. The woman who was always fine, who could take care of everyone else, had become trapped inside the very identity that once protected her.

And taking off that armor was difficult because it was not simply a habit. My body had learned it.

Familiar survival can feel much safer than unfamiliar freedom.

Performing Vulnerability

Lately I have been thinking about the difference between performing vulnerability and actually being vulnerable.

They can look surprisingly similar from the outside.

We can talk openly about our trauma.

Name our attachment patterns.

Tell people about our perfectionism.

Post something raw.

Say, “I know this is something I need to work on.”

All of those things can be meaningful.

But vulnerability asks something more difficult of us. It asks us to loosen our grip on how we are perceived. To risk being misunderstood. To allow someone to be disappointed in us. And perhaps hardest of all, vulnerability sometimes asks us to stay present when our understanding of ourselves is challenged.

Before the mind begins making its case, the body has often already sounded the alarm.

The chest tightens.

The stomach drops.

Thoughts accelerate.

The urge to explain arrives.

Suddenly we are not simply having a conversation.

We are protecting an identity.

Good person.

Competent person.

Kind person.

Successful person.

Easy person.

Whatever identity once helped us belong.

And that is where the mask becomes a cage.

We stop listening and start managing.

We stop feeling and start explaining.

We stop relating and start protecting the image of who we believe we need to be.

The tragedy is that the mask promises belonging while quietly interfering with intimacy.

I have experienced this in relationships too. Someone can tell us deeply personal things. They can share heartbreak, betrayal, loss, fear, or shame. There may be moments of extraordinary closeness.

And yet disclosure and vulnerability are not always the same thing.

Being known requires more than telling someone what happened to us. Eventually intimacy asks us to remain present. To communicate. To repair. To allow our choices to become visible. To let another person’s experience matter alongside our own.

This is where words alone stop being enough.

When the Story Becomes the Cage

The mask can also become a story.

Sometimes that story began long before we were old enough to recognize it. We were human beings adapting inside families, relationships, cultures, and systems made up of other human beings carrying their own wounds and limitations.

Something painful happens, and instead of believing:

Something painful happened to me,

we begin believing:

I am unlovable.

I am too much.

I am not enough.

People always leave.

I am better off alone.

I am broken.

The story begins as an explanation.

Then, slowly, it becomes an identity.

Once a story becomes part of our identity, we can begin organizing our lives around it without realizing what we are doing.

We notice the evidence that confirms it.

Interpret new experiences through it.

Repeat familiar dynamics because, however painful, they still make sense inside the world we already know.

Not because the original pain wasn’t real.

But because healing eventually asks us to recognize when the story that helped us survive is narrowing what we believe is possible.

One of the stories I built from my own heartbreak was that because I understood pain so deeply, I should be more patient with someone else’s.

More understanding.

More accommodating.

More willing to make room for their wounds.

For years, I believed I was the problem because I reacted to painful relationships. I thought if I could just stay calm enough, ask for little enough, never become upset, and somehow become emotionally perfect, I could finally be loved without consequence.

But I am a human being with a nervous system.

I have needs.

I have emotions.

I take up space.

Years ago, while we were walking back from one of my first graduate classes, a dear friend said something to me that I have never forgotten:

“Don’t ever let someone put your light out.”

I think I have spent years learning what that sentence actually means.

Dating and the Story of Being Broken

Dating has a remarkable way of waking old stories.

Someone pulls away.

Someone says they are not ready.

Someone cannot meet us emotionally.

Someone disappoints us.

And suddenly the mind begins searching for meaning.

Maybe I am too much.

Maybe I am not enough.

Maybe no one will ever really choose me.

Maybe I am broken.

Pain wants an explanation.

Sometimes the easiest explanation is:

It must be me.

But there is a difference between saying, I have sometimes been drawn to emotionally unavailable people, and saying, I am broken, and that is why love never works for me.

There is a difference between saying, This relationship hurt me, and saying, This proves I am unlovable.

There is a difference between discernment and self-condemnation.

I am learning that healing requires the first without requiring the second.

The Person Beneath the Mask

This most recent relationship touched something tender in me.

After seven years of being single, after years of coming back home to myself, I opened my heart again.

And that mattered.

There were moments of closeness that felt extraordinarily real. Moments when I believed I was seeing parts of another person that were not easily shown.

There was tenderness.

There was trust.

There were things shared between us that felt sacred because they were not offered casually.

I saw complexity.

I saw pain.

I saw warmth.

I saw the possibility of something deeper.

And because I had spent much of my own life behind armor, I understood something about what it means to both long for closeness and fear what closeness asks of us.

That recognition created compassion.

It also created hope.

I began believing that perhaps with enough time, enough trust, enough space, enough safety, something would shift.

There were difficult chapters of his life still unfolding.

Responsibilities.

Stress. Things that seemed to leave little room for anything else.

Part of me believed that when those burdens became lighter, his capacity for relationship would become more available.

But eventually I had to confront something I did not want to confront:

Seeing someone’s capacity in moments is not the same thing as experiencing that capacity consistently.

And loving the person I believed I could glimpse beneath someone’s defenses was not the same thing as being in relationship with the person who was actually available to me.

That may be one of the hardest distinctions I have ever had to accept.

I did not need to decide who he really was underneath everything.

I needed to pay attention to who I was becoming while I waited.

When Compassion Becomes Rationalization

I know something about staying because I understand someone’s pain.

I have done it before.

When I left my marriage, I learned one of the most painful lessons of my life:

Love is not always enough to make a relationship safe.

I remember standing in the driveway, caught between letting my husband return home and protecting the two children inside. Only a week earlier, they had watched him be taken away by police because he was so intoxicated that the situation had become unsafe.

I understood that he was hurting.

I understood that there was trauma underneath what was happening.

I had compassion for him.

But standing there, something in me finally became clear.

Understanding his pain was not changing what was happening.

Loving him was not making us safer.

And the way I had been loving him had begun keeping all of us inside a cycle that was slowly destroying us.

For years, staying had looked like devotion. Loyalty. Unconditional love.

Leaving forced me to grieve not only the relationship, but everything I wanted it to become.

The intact family.

The fairy-tale ending.

Growing old beside someone.

The belief that if I loved hard enough, waited long enough, understood deeply enough, or remained devoted enough, something would finally change.

Eventually I understood:

His healing did not belong to me.

That realization changed the way I understood love.

Unconditional love does not mean unconditional access.

I can love another human being and still refuse to participate in the destruction of either of us.

And yet years later, I found myself brushing against a softer version of the same lesson. Not the same relationship. Not the same circumstances. But a familiar internal movement.

Waiting.

Understanding.

Explaining.

Hoping.

Believing there might be more capacity later. When work became less demanding. When responsibilities changed. When difficult chapters finally closed.

But “later” was never something I had actually been promised.

It was a story I was creating because I needed the waiting to make sense.

And then my body began showing me something my hope did not want to see.

I started editing myself. I hesitated before sharing my stress because I worried my needs would become another burden. I adjusted myself around someone else’s availability. I accepted apologies that were not followed by meaningful change. I listened closely to explanations because I wanted them to be true.

And somewhere along the way, compassion had started becoming rationalization.

That was the moment the story began to change.

Return to What Is Actually Here

One of the hardest parts of healing is discovering that insight does not automatically make us free.

I could understand attachment.

Understand trauma.

Understand nervous systems.

Understand avoidance.

Understand survival strategies.

Understand his pain.

Understand mine.

And still abandon myself.

Awareness matters. But eventually awareness has to become embodied.

I had to stop asking only: Why might this be happening?

and begin asking: What is happening?

What happens inside my body when communication disappears?

What happens to me when consistency becomes uncertain?

What happens when I keep hoping for a relationship that does not exist in the form I want it to?

Who do I become while I am waiting?

I had to stop measuring the relationship by its most intimate moments and look at the whole of it.

His time with me had decreased.

I remained separate from much of his life.

The words and behavior did not consistently align.

And finally I stopped trying to solve the confusion.

Not because I suddenly understood everything.

Because I no longer needed to.

Reality did not require a diagnosis.

It required my attention.

What was available to me was not what I desired.

That did not make him bad.

It did not make what we shared meaningless.

And it did not make me unworthy.

It simply meant I had to stop loving possibility more than I was listening to reality.

The Mirror

Relationships become mirrors.

They show us where we have healed.

Where we still ache.

What we are willing to tolerate.

Where old stories are trying to reclaim us.

Sometimes the greatest evidence of healing is not finally finding someone who never hurts us. Sometimes it is recognizing that when reality becomes clear, we no longer abandon ourselves in order to keep the relationship.

This relationship reflected something important back to me. Two people can care for one another and still not have what is required to build something reciprocal and lasting.

Compassion without boundaries can quietly become self-abandonment.

My capacity to love deeply is not the problem. It is one of the things I value most about myself.

But love needs somewhere to land.

Without reciprocity, love can become waiting. Overgiving. Explaining. Hoping. Editing.

I no longer want to confuse chemistry with compatibility. I no longer want “the spark” to be enough reason to remain somewhere my nervous system is continually negotiating for security.

Reciprocity feels different in my body now.

Less intoxicating, perhaps.

More spacious.

More peaceful.

There is room to breathe.

There is room to speak.

There is room for both people to exist.

I want a relationship in which two people can see and be seen. Where behavior can carry the weight of words. Where care is not something I have to infer from rare moments of closeness.

Hope can keep us tied to potential for a very long time.

But potential is not partnership.

Accountability Is Not Shame

This is where I believe reclamation begins.

Not in blaming the other person.

And not in blaming ourselves.

In becoming willing to see our own participation.

Accountability is not shame. Accountability is agency.

It is the moment we recognize that while we may not have chosen what happened to us, we have choices in what we do next.

I can ask:

What am I participating in?

What am I tolerating because it feels familiar?

Where am I abandoning myself to preserve connection?

Where am I staying silent because I am afraid honesty will cost me someone?

Where am I asking another person to prove that I am lovable?

Where am I using another person’s limitations as evidence that something is wrong with me?

Those are not shame questions.

They are reclamation questions.

Compassion can help us understand why the cage was built. But compassion alone does not open the door.

Eventually we have to be willing to see the story differently. To notice the armor. To tolerate the discomfort of honesty. To risk being seen without performing.

And perhaps most importantly, to ask:

How am I participating in this story?

Not because everything is my fault.

Because the moment I can recognize my participation is also the moment choice returns.

Choosing Myself Without Making Him the Villain

Choosing myself does not mean deciding that none of it mattered. It does not require making him bad so that I can leave. And it does not require pretending I am no longer hurt.

I miss him.

I miss the nightly flirting.

I miss sharing pieces of my day and hearing about his.

I miss the friendship.

I miss the sex.

I miss the closeness.

And perhaps most painfully, I miss the hope.

There was a version of the future I allowed myself to imagine after years of not imagining one with anyone.

Letting go of a person is painful. Letting go of the future we imagined with them can be its own kind of grief.

And somewhere inside me, there is still a tender part that wanted my absence to matter enough to change something.

She wanted to be chosen.

I have compassion for her. Wanting to be chosen is deeply human.

But reclamation is teaching me that being chosen by another person cannot be the price of choosing myself.

I can miss him and still choose myself.

I can wish something had unfolded differently and still choose myself.

I can believe there was love between us and still choose myself.

I can honor the tenderness I saw without building my life around the hope of what it might one day become.

Perhaps that is where I am now.

Not hardened.

Not bitter.

Not pretending it did not matter.

Just learning to allow what was real to be real without demanding that it become more than it was.

I opened my heart after years of protecting it. That matters too.

I loved authentically.

I stayed soft.

And even though this hurts, I do not want to make my capacity to love the thing I regret.

I want to protect it.

Honor it.

Trust it.

And the next time I offer that kind of love, I want to pay closer attention to whether there is someone there who can meet it.

The mask was not a failure. It was an intelligent way of protecting belonging, safety, identity, or connection. What once protected you does not have to control you forever. And someone else’s story does not have to become yours.

This week’s practice

Remember

The mask was not a failure. It was an intelligent way of protecting belonging, safety, identity, or connection. The story was not foolish either. It may have been the only way you knew to make sense of what hurt.

You do not have to hate the version of yourself who adapted. You can thank her. You can understand her. And you can begin noticing when the protection she created no longer belongs to the life you are trying to build.

What once protected you does not have to control you forever. And someone else’s story does not have to become yours.

Return

When you notice yourself becoming defensive, overly agreeable, polished, distant, or performative, try pausing before you explain.

Notice your body. Where is there tightening? Heat? Pressure? Numbness? A desire to run, fix, defend, please, or disappear?

You might gently ask:

“What am I trying to protect right now? What story am I beginning to tell myself? What am I afraid this moment says about me?”

Then give the experience somewhere to go. Write. Draw. Scribble. Move. Walk outside. Place a hand over the part of your body asking to be noticed.

There is nothing you have to interpret correctly. The practice is simply returning long enough to hear yourself.

Reclaim

Reclamation does not always look dramatic. Sometimes it looks like a breath before the performance begins. An “I don’t know.” An “I got defensive.” An “I care more than I want to admit.” An “I understand why this is hard for you, and this still isn’t working for me.” An “I know what I need here.”

Sometimes reclaiming ourselves means refusing to turn rejection into evidence that we are broken. Sometimes it means loving another person deeply while recognizing that their healing does not belong to us. Sometimes it means accepting that we saw something beautiful and real in someone and still could not build a life with the version of them who was available.

And sometimes reclamation is quieter than all of that. Sometimes it is simply noticing that, somewhere in the process of trying to understand another person’s cage, we have begun climbing back into one of our own.

And choosing, gently, to step back out.
