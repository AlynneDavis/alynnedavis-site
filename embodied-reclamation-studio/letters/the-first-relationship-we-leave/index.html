Letters from the Studio

The First Relationship We Leave

On self-abandonment, empathy, and the quiet ways we learn to leave ourselves in order to belong.

Before we know our names, we know ourselves.
Before we know who the world expects us to become, we already know who we are.

A newborn doesn’t wonder whether they’re too much.

They cry when they’re hungry. They reach to be held. They laugh from somewhere deep in their belly. They don’t apologize for taking up space. They don’t question whether they’re worthy of comfort or connection. They simply trust what their bodies are telling them.

Long before they understand language, they know themselves through sensation, instinct, and relationship.

Lately, I’ve been wondering if embodiment isn’t something we have to learn at all.

What if it’s something we slowly forget?

Not because we choose to.

Because life quietly teaches us to adapt.

Little by little, we begin learning which parts of ourselves are welcomed and which parts feel risky to reveal. We discover that some emotions make the people we love uncomfortable. We learn that some needs are easier to silence than express. We become experts at reading everyone else before we’ve ever learned to read ourselves.

We don’t stop having needs.

We simply stop trusting that it’s safe to honor them.

I don’t believe we are born disconnected from ourselves.

I believe disconnection is something we learn in the service of survival.

When the people or systems we depend on cannot fully meet us in our pain, our nervous systems do something remarkably intelligent.

They adapt.

We become quieter.

More agreeable.

More independent.

More attuned to everyone else’s experience than our own.

Not because we are broken.

Because we are trying to belong.

Over time, those adaptations become so familiar that we mistake them for our personality when, in reality, they began as protection.

Looking back, I wonder if the first relationship many of us leave isn’t with another person at all.

It’s the relationship with ourselves.

I learned from a young age how to become the “good girl.”

The overachiever.

The peacemaker.

The one who could read a room before anyone else had spoken.

I wanted my mother to be happy.

I wanted to be beautiful because beauty was praised.

I wanted to be smart because achievement was celebrated.

Somewhere along the way, I became convinced that being loved had something to do with becoming whatever the people around me needed.

I don’t remember deciding that everyone else’s feelings mattered more than mine.

I only remember becoming very good at noticing them.

One of my earliest memories is sitting in church, acutely aware that my mother was watching me.

She wasn’t always sitting beside me, but somehow I always knew where she was. Every so often I’d glance across the sanctuary and catch her eyes fixed on me.

If I wasn’t singing the hymns with enough enthusiasm…

If my mind wandered during the sermon…

If my body, so full of restless energy that I now understand was ADHD, couldn’t stay still…

I knew that look.

It wasn’t anger.

It was disappointment.

Sometimes frustration.

Sometimes something that quietly settled into shame.

Nothing had to be said.

Her eyes communicated everything.

I knew a conversation would come later.

Looking back, I don’t believe my mother intended to teach me to leave myself. She was parenting from her own story, her own fears, and the beliefs she had inherited.

But children don’t experience our intentions.

They experience our nervous systems.

Without realizing it, I began practicing a skill that would follow me into adulthood. Becoming exquisitely attuned to everyone else’s expectations, while slowly losing touch with my own experience.

The moment I caught her eyes, my body changed before my thoughts did.

My shoulders stiffened.

I stopped moving.

I scanned myself.

Was my dress wrinkled?

Was I sitting correctly?

Was anyone else looking?

My body learned to monitor itself before anyone else had to.

Long before I learned to trust myself…

I learned to watch myself.

I didn’t know it then, but this was one of the first ways I learned to leave myself. My attention was no longer anchored inside my own body. It was anchored in the eyes of someone else. Safety wasn’t found by asking, What do I feel? It was found by asking, Am I doing this right?

I’ve been thinking about all of this lately because of a relationship that entered my life with so much hope.

It wasn’t simply the relationship that changed me.

It was who I noticed myself becoming inside of it.

For the first time in my life, I intentionally practiced something I had spent decades trying to avoid.

I asked for what I needed.

That sounds simple.

It wasn’t.

Every vulnerable conversation awakened something ancient inside me.

I remember sitting with my phone, trying to compose the message I knew I needed to send. It was a message that said I needed to step back in order to honor myself and my needs.

I rewrote it over and over again.

Not because I didn’t know what I wanted to say.

But because I was trying to find a version of myself that couldn’t be rejected.

As I stared at the screen, one thought kept looping through my mind.

Why do I have to do this again?

Why do I always have to be the one who chooses myself?

Why can’t this just be easy?

Why can’t I simply stay and accept things as they are?

My stomach tightened into that familiar knot only fear seems to know how to tie. It was the kind of nausea that burns from the inside out.

My body knew what was happening long before my mind caught up.

I copied the message into an editing app.

Changing words.

Softening sentences.

Removing anything that sounded too emotional.

Too needy.

Too demanding.

I wanted to leave the door slightly open.

I wanted to make sure they knew I understood their perspective.

Looking back…

I wasn’t editing a text.

I was editing myself.

I was trying to become someone whose needs would be easier to love.

For the first time…

I stopped watching myself long enough to listen to myself.

That quieter voice inside me had been there all along.

I just hadn’t trusted it.

Inside me, I could hear my younger self pleading…

Don’t send it.

You might lose your chance at happiness.

You’ll be rejected again.

You’ll be alone again.

Then another voice emerged.

It wasn’t loud.

It wasn’t dramatic.

It was simply wiser.

The part of me I had spent years trying to return to.

It gently asked,

What if choosing yourself isn’t what destroys the relationship? What if it’s what finally stops destroying your relationship with yourself?

With shaking hands, I pressed send.

Then I waited.

Not just for a response.

For a verdict.

If they reject me…

What does that mean about me?

Will it prove I’m not enough?

Not lovable enough?

Not worthy enough to be chosen?

As I sat with those questions, I realized something that surprised me.

The fear of rejection hadn’t simply shaped my relationships.

It had shaped my identity.

For years, it had quietly become the compass beneath so many of my decisions. Pulling me toward accommodation instead of authenticity. Toward belonging instead of embodiment.

Maybe that was one of the deepest ways I had learned to leave myself.

The grief felt ancient.

It wasn’t only the grief of this relationship.

It was the grief of realizing how many times I had abandoned myself long before anyone else had the chance.

But what surprised me most wasn’t my fear of rejection.

It was how quickly my empathy wanted to rescue me from my own truth.

I’ve always considered empathy one of my greatest gifts. It allows me to recognize the pain beneath someone’s behavior and remain compassionate, even when it’s difficult.

But I began realizing something I had never considered before.

Empathy, without embodiment, can quietly become another form of self-abandonment.

Long before I had language for trauma, attachment, or nervous systems, empathy was how I learned to move through the world.

I was the child who walked laps around the playground with the girl everyone teased because she was pigeon-toed. I wanted her to know she wasn’t alone.

I invited the little girl from the trailer park to my birthday party because everyone else saw her worn-out shoes. I saw her loneliness.

One day, a classmate brought magazines she had found hidden under her father’s bed to school. When the teacher discovered them, I volunteered to take the punishment. Even then, something inside me knew she didn’t need more shame. She needed someone to stand beside her.

Looking back…

I don’t think I was simply being kind.

I think I was becoming the protector I wished someone had been for me.

Every child I rescued carried a piece of something I recognized inside myself.

It took me years to realize that empathy had become the way I learned to stay connected before I learned how to stay connected to myself.

I didn’t become empathic because I was a therapist.

I became a therapist because empathy had been my first language.

The same empathy that helped me protect others sometimes kept me from protecting myself.

I became so connected to another person’s experience…

that I slowly lost connection with my own.

I could understand their perspective before I had fully listened to mine.

I could make space for their fears before acknowledging my own.

I wasn’t disappearing because someone else was asking me to. I was disappearing because my nervous system had learned long ago that preserving connection sometimes meant leaving myself.

And maybe that’s the most compassionate truth I’ve discovered.

My empathy wasn’t the problem.

My empathy simply arrived before my own experience did.

Can I remain in relationship with myself while staying in relationship with another?

I realized that every relationship before this one had quietly been asking me a different question.

Who do I need to become to keep this connection?

This was the first time I was asking something entirely different.

Who might I become if I refused to leave myself?

Can I stay?

Can I tell the truth?

Can I disappoint someone… and stay?

Can I survive misunderstanding… and stay?

Can I love… without disappearing?

Can I belong without abandoning myself?

The relationship wasn’t asking me to abandon myself.

It was revealing how practiced I already was at doing it.

Children don’t choose authenticity over attachment.

If authenticity threatens connection… they choose connection.

Every time.

Not because they are weak.

Because they are brilliantly designed for survival.

Healing isn’t about criticizing our adaptations. It’s about recognizing that what once protected us may no longer be leading us where we want to go.

Our adaptations don’t deserve our shame.

They deserve our gratitude.

And eventually… our permission to rest.

I still notice myself rewriting the occasional text. I still feel my chest tighten when I have to say something vulnerable.

I still hear that younger part of me whisper,

Don’t send it.

Don’t risk it.

Don’t lose them.

She still believes belonging depends on becoming smaller.

I don’t blame her anymore.

She learned that in the only way a child could.

But now another voice has begun speaking too.

It reminds me that I can survive disappointment.

I can survive misunderstanding.

I can even survive heartbreak.

What I no longer want to survive…

is the slow erosion of leaving myself.

No relationship is worth abandoning the one I’ve spent a lifetime trying to reclaim.

I used to believe the greatest risk in love was being abandoned.

I’m beginning to wonder if the greater loss is abandoning ourselves before anyone else has the chance.

Perhaps that’s what embodiment has been teaching me all along.

Not how to stop needing connection.

Not how to stop loving deeply.

But how to remain in relationship with myself while staying open to relationship with another.

Maybe healing isn’t becoming fearless.

Maybe it’s trusting my body again.

Trusting the tightening in my chest.

Trusting the ache in my stomach.

Trusting the quiet voice that whispers, This matters.

Because before I learned to watch myself…

I knew how to be myself.

Before I learned to belong by becoming someone else…

I already belonged to myself.

And perhaps that is what embodiment has been asking of me all along.

Not to become someone new.

But to remember the first relationship I ever had.

The one I never truly lost, only learned to leave.
