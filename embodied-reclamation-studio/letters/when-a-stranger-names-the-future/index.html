Letters from the Studio, Letter Five

When a Stranger Names the Future

On the warnings we cannot yet receive, the stories we mistake for love, and the slow return to ourselves.

Standing in line on Christmas Eve Eve, I had my hair curled into that perfect Old Hollywood glam style. I wore control-top pantyhose because I had given birth to our son only six weeks earlier, and a very short dress that showed off my legs because that was what my fiancé wanted.

On my feet were black patent leather shoes with velvet ties that formed perfect little bows. In my hands, I held a bouquet of miniature pink roses, pink has always been my favorite color, wrapped in ribbon by a woman at the grocery store who had graciously helped us as we hurried to the magistrate’s office to get married.

I had assembled all the visible evidence of a happy bride.

The hair.

The dress.

The flowers.

The shoes.

The photographs we would take.

Some part of me seemed to believe that if I could make the day look beautiful enough, perhaps I could make myself feel what I thought a bride was supposed to feel.

But underneath all that beauty, my body was telling a different story.

My stomach was full of butterflies, but beneath them was a pit, an uneasiness that did not match the excitement I thought I should be feeling. By then, the sensation was already deeply familiar to me. I had lived beside it for so long that I had learned to interpret it as evidence of intense, passionate love.

I did not yet understand that familiarity does not always mean safety.

Sometimes we live beside a feeling for so long that we stop treating it like a warning.

So I shrugged off the unease and reminded myself that getting married was the right thing to do.

We had two children now: our beautiful two-year-old daughter and an adorable six-week-old son. Marriage seemed like a way to make everything legitimate somehow. It would make up for having children out of wedlock. It might help me atone for all the ways I believed I had disappointed the people I loved.

Maybe my parents would finally be proud of me.

Maybe I would finally feel loved and accepted.

Maybe, if I could make our family look whole, I would finally feel whole too.

There was a religious story woven through that longing as well.

I had been taught something very particular about what a good Christian family was supposed to look like, and I knew I was not living inside that picture. My parents and sibling seemed to belong to the intact-family club I had somehow fallen outside of. I had already been divorced once. Now I was living with the father of my children without being married, carrying the shame of what I had been taught to call sin alongside the very real love I had for my family.

Marriage seemed to offer more than commitment.

It offered absolution.

Perhaps this was how I would finally get my life right. Perhaps marriage could erase the failure of my first marriage, legitimize the family I had now, and return me to the version of myself I believed God, my family, and the world could approve of.

I wanted belonging so badly that I was willing to mistake conformity for safety.

The Question Before the Wedding

The day before the wedding, I called my sister and asked her to bring her nice camera so she could help us capture the moment.

She asked me a simple question.

“Are you sure you want to marry him?”

Her words landed in my chest with a heavy, visceral thud.

For a moment, I froze.

I had felt that same freezing before when I thought about marrying him, but this time her question felt almost like an opening, a door I had secretly been waiting for someone else to unlock.

And still, I could not allow myself to walk through it.

“Well,” I said, “I think so.”

Not yes.

I think so.

Looking back, I hear so much in those three words.

My shame answered before my wisest self could. By then, that truer voice had become faint, buried beneath years of trying to hold relationships together, make the right choices, and become the version of myself I thought would finally be worthy of love.

I began explaining to myself why everything would be different.

He had been a lot better lately. He was not drinking. He was trying to work and contributing more to the bills. He seemed more willing to help with the children.

At the time, these things felt like evidence that our relationship was finally becoming what I had hoped it could be.

I did not yet recognize how low the threshold had become.

I was measuring love by improvement rather than by safety.

At least he was trying.

That sentence carried enormous weight for me then.

He was trying to stay sober. Trying to work. Trying to be more present. And he knew how to tell me the things I most desperately wanted to believe: that he had missed me, that he had missed our daughter, that he knew he had messed up, that he wanted something better for himself and for our children.

And I wanted desperately for this time to be different.

This time it will stick.

This time he really means it.

My heart kept reaching for a future for which I had very little evidence because I wanted so badly to have the dream, the support, the partnership, the family I imagined marriage would somehow create.

Denial became its own kind of anesthesia.

It allowed hope to remain intact even when reality kept asking harder questions.

What “Better” Had Come to Mean

The bar had moved so gradually that I had barely noticed.

By then, help with the children could mean simply being physically present in the house.

I can still picture him standing at the end of the bar with our newborn tucked into the carrier my mother had lovingly sewn by hand, his computer open in front of him while he was “working.” He was there, technically. But even in that memory, vivid enough that I can still see it as if it happened yesterday, his attention was somewhere else.

He was always chasing the next big break, the next business idea, the thing that might finally change everything.

And somehow I had become satisfied with presence without presence.

He was home.

He was not at a bar.

For a while, I allowed that to be enough.

I had lowered the threshold so gradually that I could experience someone’s physical proximity as evidence of partnership.

Maybe that is one of the quieter ways we lose track of ourselves, not all at once, but by adjusting our definition of enough until almost anything can begin to resemble hope.

Chosen and Claimed

The day before I stood in that line to say “I do,” I had been sitting at our kitchen table breastfeeding our newborn son when my fiancé announced,

“We are getting married tomorrow, and you are not getting out of it this time.”

In the haze of postpartum hormones, exhaustion, and sleeplessness, something about those words sounded romantic to me.

He was choosing me.

Or at least, that was the story I knew how to tell myself.

For years, I had longed for a family that felt whole and permanent. I wanted a home where everyone belonged to one another. I wanted to be chosen with certainty.

His declaration seemed to promise all of that.

And yet there was something else inside the words.

He was not only choosing me.

He was claiming me.

There is a difference I could not yet understand.

Being claimed felt intoxicating because I had spent so much of my life longing to feel wanted. There was something almost reassuring about imagining myself as valuable enough to be treasured, protected, placed on a pedestal.

But pedestals are precarious places.

They offer admiration only as long as you remain where you have been placed.

Somewhere inside me, I already knew that if I disappointed him, resisted him, or became someone other than the woman he wanted me to be, I could fall from that pedestal just as quickly as I had been placed upon it.

What had taught me to confuse possession with devotion?

Even as one part of me heard his declaration as romance, another part reacted immediately.

Anxiety rushed through my veins.

It almost felt like excitement, but beneath the buzz was something colder and much older: the awareness that I was not entirely safe.

I did not experience saying no as a real option.

I am not sure I could have explained that clearly then. My mind still had stories about love and loyalty and family. But my body understood something my conscious mind had not yet found language for.

Saying no felt dangerous.

So I said yes almost automatically, as though agreement could quiet the liquid-ice fear running through me.

And intertwined with that fear was relief.

I had finally been chosen by another human being, or at least that was how I understood it then. Some part of me believed his choosing could give me the self-acceptance I had been unable to give myself.

I had a place now.

A role.

A name for who I belonged to.

Marriage would legitimize me.

Perhaps, for a moment, even my nervous system could confuse that certainty with safety.

What had taught me that someone’s intense fascination with me was the same thing as being loved safely?

This was not the first time he had asked me to marry him.

He had proposed twice before.

And somewhere inside me, I already understood that saying no again would carry a cost.

Boundaries had rarely been received simply as boundaries. A no could become an argument, an injury, an accusation, a withdrawal, something I would eventually be asked to repair.

So when he said, “You are not getting out of it this time,” there was fear in me.

But there was relief too.

He had made the decision.

I would not have to.

And beneath all of that lived a much younger version of me, the teenage girl who had watched Dance ’Til Dawn more times than I could count and still believed, somewhere deep inside, that love could deliver the fairytale ending.

I wanted the story where the complicated man became certain.

Where his intensity meant devotion.

Where being pursued meant being precious.

Where all the difficulty before the ending merely proved how much the ending mattered.

I did not yet understand how differently safe love feels.

Safe love does not require us to surrender our no in order to preserve someone else’s yes.

Momentum

And then everything began moving very quickly.

We got the marriage license. I went shopping for a dress. I found a wedding band that was affordable and could be sized the same day.

I moved from task to task with a kind of frantic efficiency.

At the time, I called it excitement.

Looking back, it felt more like momentum.

I knew how to perform the mechanics of a wedding. I had been married before. I had helped friends plan their weddings. I knew which boxes needed to be checked.

Dress.

Ring.

Flowers.

Witness.

Photographs.

Each completed task made the wedding feel more inevitable.

I called a dear friend and asked her to be our witness. She was delighted for me, warm and supportive in exactly the way a loving friend would be when told someone she cared about was getting married.

Her excitement should have made me feel more certain.

Instead, it made my unease harder to acknowledge.

Everyone around me seemed to be participating in the story of a wedding. I wanted desperately to find my way inside that story too.

But instead of excitement, I felt strangely mechanical.

My body moved. I made phone calls. I shopped. I organized. I cared for my newborn son through the fog of sleepless nights.

I pumped breast milk between trying on clothes so I could protect my milk supply and keep from becoming engorged. I answered calls and texts. I rushed from one task to the next.

There was no quiet.

No empty room.

No long drive alone.

No ten minutes in which someone asked nothing of me and I could hear what was happening inside myself.

There was simply the next thing to do.

And then the next.

And then the next.

It felt as though someone else were moving my body through the day while I watched from somewhere far away.

Momentum can begin to feel a lot like consent when there is no space left to stop.

The Stranger in the Line

By the time we arrived at the magistrate’s office, I looked every bit the bride.

That was when the warning came.

My fiancé had stepped away, and our small wedding party was talking among themselves when I noticed the woman standing directly behind me.

I was difficult to miss. I seemed to be the only person in the room dressed for a wedding and holding flowers.

Most of the other women were dressed casually. Some looked exhausted or frightened. Many held paperwork.

I held roses.

They held documents from the court.

At the time, I believed the bouquet separated me from them.

I thought I was there for an entirely different reason.

I was entering marriage.

They seemed to be trying to escape something.

I did not yet understand how close those two stories could live beside one another.

The woman and I began making small talk. I have always been curious about people and rarely meet a stranger, so eventually I asked what had brought her there.

She told me she was filing for a restraining order.

My heart squeezed.

I felt warmth rise into my face, and I told her how sorry I was that she was going through that.

Her answer also exposed something in me that I am not especially proud of.

I had already made assumptions about her based on what she looked like and where we were standing. Her story surprised me because somewhere inside me, I apparently believed I knew what a woman who needed protection would look like.

And she did not look like me.

Or perhaps more accurately, I needed to believe she did not.

I was educated. I was independent. I had worked for myself for years. I was articulate. I was strong.

I was also trained professionally in clinical mental health.

Surely awareness itself would protect me.

Surely knowing the terminology meant I would recognize danger if it entered my own life.

Surely unhealthy relationships happened to some other kind of woman.

There is a strange protection in believing ourselves to be the exception.

I could see her vulnerability immediately.

I could feel compassion for her without hesitation.

The irony would take me years to understand:

I could recognize her need for safety much more easily than I could recognize my own.

Then she looked directly into my eyes.

Something in her face changed.

There was a certainty there that startled me.

“One day, you will be back here in this line for one of these.”

For a moment, I could not speak.

It was as though the air had been sucked from the room.

I could hear my heartbeat pounding against my chest like a drum. Everything around me seemed to recede.

For a few seconds, time felt suspended.

The room seemed to grow smaller around us.

Heat gathered beneath my arms. My palms turned clammy, and I tightened my grip around the stems of the miniature pink roses as though the bouquet could anchor me to the story I had walked in carrying.

Bride.

Wedding.

Happy.

Her face was dark and solemn.

There was no teasing in her expression, no hesitation that I can remember. Her words landed flat and blunt in my ears.

“One day, you will be back here in this line for one of these.”

Had she seen something between us?

Had she recognized something in my face?

Or was she speaking from the hard-earned knowing of her own life?

I will never know.

What I do know is that my body reacted before I could construct an argument against her.

Underneath the shock, beneath the embarrassment, offense, and disbelief, there was something else.

A deep inner knowing.

Some part of me had heard her completely.

I laughed a little, but underneath the laugh I felt scared and embarrassed.

How could she possibly think I belonged in the same category as her?

I was the bride.

I had flowers in my hands.

I was getting married.

And yet somewhere beneath my indignation was another feeling I did not want to touch.

Recognition.

Some part of me knew she was not entirely wrong.

I thought she was placing me into her story.

I did not yet understand that perhaps she was recognizing herself in mine.

So I brushed her off with a dismissive, slightly snarky response.

“Well, I sure hope not, since this is my wedding day.”

My body tightened almost immediately.

I felt myself clench like a ball made from hundreds of rubber bands pulled too tight.

I remember hoping the line would move quickly so I would not have to keep standing beside her.

I searched for my fiancé.

I turned toward my friends.

I tried to return to the story I had been living in only minutes before.

Bride.

Wedding.

Family.

Happily ever after.

I wanted distance from the woman who had frightened me, though she was not the person my body feared.

What frightened me was the sensation of being seen.

It felt almost like being stripped bare in the middle of a town square, exposed before I was ready to admit there was anything to expose.

Perhaps that was why offense was easier to tolerate than terror.

If I could make her rude, inappropriate, presumptuous, then I did not have to wonder whether she had noticed something I was spending enormous energy trying not to notice myself.

Why was I more frightened by being seen than by what she might have seen?

What I Did Not Say

I never told anyone what she had said.

Not my sister.

Not my friends.

Not my fiancé.

Some irrational part of me believed that repeating the warning might somehow make it come true. If I did not say it aloud, perhaps I could leave it there in the courthouse beside the stranger who had spoken it.

There may have been another reason for my silence too.

Saying it aloud would have made it real.

Someone might have asked me another question.

Someone might have noticed my hesitation.

Someone might have given me permission to stop.

And then I would have had to decide whether I was willing, or able, to take it.

So I stayed quiet.

I told myself the woman had said something strange and inappropriate. I told myself it meant nothing. I told myself it was only a coincidence.

But beneath every explanation was the knowing I could not quite silence.

She might be right.

Silence kept me from having to choose between the truth and the life I was still trying to preserve.

Then my name was called.

I walked forward.

I said, “I do.”

I barely remember him wrapping an arm around my waist while I smiled involuntarily for the camera.

The Wedding Night

For the rest of the evening, I remember feeling as though I were moving through a dream.

We went for sushi at my favorite restaurant. It was casual and ordinary in a way that almost seemed impossible after the emotional intensity of the day.

And then we went home.

I tried to make my body feel excited.

I tried to make myself feel sexual.

I tried to become the new wife I imagined my husband expected me to be on our wedding night.

Even after the ceremony, I was still trying to make my body participate in a story it did not trust.

What I really wanted was to hold my baby.

I wanted softness.

Comfort.

Rest.

But I had absorbed another story about marriage, one rooted in what I had been taught about two people becoming “one flesh” and about a wife’s responsibility not to deny her husband sexual intimacy.

Somewhere along the way, I had learned that marriage changed the ownership of my own no.

My body no longer seemed entirely mine to listen to.

I had not yet learned that commitment does not erase consent.

Marriage did not make my body less mine.

A vow did not turn discomfort into obligation.

But those truths had not yet become available to me in a way I could live.

So even on the night I became a wife, I practiced the same thing I had practiced for years: leaving myself in order to remain connected.

Some part of me knew I was not safe.

Another part of me was working desperately not to know.

For a long time, I interpreted moments like this as personal failures.

Why didn’t I listen to myself?

Why didn’t I leave?

Why didn’t I say no when my sister asked whether I was sure?

But those questions flatten the complexity of what happens inside us when attachment, fear, shame, hope, love, survival, and longing become tangled together.

Leaving myself had once helped me preserve relationships.

Silencing my fear allowed me to remain connected to people I was terrified of losing. Performing happiness helped keep other people comfortable. Ignoring my body’s warnings allowed me to keep believing that if I loved enough, tried enough, forgave enough, or became good enough, the relationship might finally become safe.

I did not yet understand how much that survival strategy was costing me.

And I certainly did not know that the stranger standing behind me in that courthouse line would become part of the story I would carry for years.

What Happened to Her Words?

For years, I carried her words with me.

I tried not to.

I could dismiss the woman intellectually. I could tell myself she had been inappropriate, presumptuous, even cruel. But I could not completely forget what happened inside my body when she spoke.

Not long after we were married, there was a night when my husband became angry and threw his laptop across the room.

I was holding our son.

The computer came toward us and struck so close to my ankle that I remember the terror before I remember almost anything else.

My body went immediately into protection.

Hold the baby.

Shield him.

Don’t move the wrong way.

Don’t make this worse.

And somewhere in that terror, the courthouse returned.

The woman.

Her face.

Her voice.

“One day, you will be back here in this line for one of these.”

Her words echoed through my mind while I held my child.

I did not leave that night.

That matters to say.

Recognition did not suddenly give me the ability to reorganize my life.

Fear did not automatically create freedom.

Knowing did not instantly untangle attachment, children, hope, shame, finances, family, love, responsibility, and all the other threads that had become woven together.

But her sentence had found a place inside me.

Every time something happened that frightened me, it became a little harder to convince myself that she had imagined what I could not yet bear to see.

Years later, I would understand something important.

She had not created the future by naming it.

She had recognized something that was already present.

The Second Line

Years later, I stood in another line.

I had become the woman the bride with the pink roses believed she could never be.

And I was standing in the exact same courthouse.

This time there were no Old Hollywood curls.

No short wedding dress.

No black patent leather shoes tied with velvet bows.

I wore jeans and sneakers.

And there were no roses in my hands.

There was paperwork.

Paperwork I had filled out carefully with the help of my sister, who was a family law attorney.

I remember sitting and listening to her while anger and tears seemed to come from the same place inside me. My hands shook so badly I could barely hold the pen steady. At other moments, I felt completely numb, as though I were describing someone else’s life.

Line by line, I wrote down the moments I had spent years minimizing.

The times my husband had put me in danger.

The times he had harmed me.

The experiences I had explained away, forgiven, survived, or tried desperately to forget.

Now they had to become sentences on a page.

Before coming to the courthouse, I had already gone back to the house with a police escort to get the children.

My father had taken them home with him.

And then I went to the courthouse.

The moment I walked inside, something in my body recognized the place before my mind had fully caught up.

I recognized the marble floor.

I recognized the way the glaring fluorescent lights reflected against it.

And then I remembered the roses.

I stood there feeling almost like a statue, trying to keep myself steady.

And I remembered her.

The memory was so visceral that chills traveled up my back and the hairs on my arms stood on end.

Years had passed, but suddenly the bride and the woman holding legal paperwork seemed to be standing in the same room.

The first time I had stood there, I held miniature pink roses and believed they distinguished me from the women holding court documents.

Now the documents were in my hands.

There is a version of this story I could tell as humiliation.

She warned me.

I should have listened.

I should have known.

But that is no longer the story I want to tell.

Because the woman standing in that courthouse the second time was not evidence of the bride’s failure.

She was evidence of something else.

She had done what the bride could not yet do.

She had gathered her children.

She had asked for help.

She had written down what happened.

She had allowed other people to see it.

And she had walked back into the courthouse carrying the truth in her hands.

The first time, I went there hoping marriage would make me legitimate.

The second time, I went there beginning to understand that I had never needed another person to legitimize my existence.

I needed safety.

I needed agency.

I needed my own life returned to me.

Letting the Two Women Meet

When I picture the young bride now, I do not want to shake her and ask why she didn’t listen.

I want to sit beside her.

I want to reach for the young mother in the curled hair and the too-short dress, exhausted from nursing a six-week-old baby and desperately trying to make herself look as happy as she believed she ought to feel.

I want to hold her while she weeps.

I want to tell her that she is valuable before anyone chooses her.

That she is worthy of a love that does not frighten her.

That another person’s desire for her does not determine her worth.

That she is allowed to have boundaries even when someone else is disappointed by them.

That no one else has to live inside the consequences of her life simply because they have an opinion about what that life should look like.

Mostly, I want to protect her from all the pain I know is coming.

Of course I cannot.

That may be one of the hardest parts of looking backward with compassion.

We cannot travel back and rescue the selves we once were.

But perhaps we can stop abandoning them in memory.

I no longer need to make that young bride foolish so that the woman I am now can be wise.

She was doing the best she knew how to do with the safety, language, attachment, beliefs, and choices available to her then.

She wanted what so many of us want.

To belong.

To be loved.

To know someone would stay.

She just had not yet learned that staying and safety are not the same thing.

And perhaps the woman I am today does not need to forgive her for failing to know better.

Perhaps she was never the one who needed forgiving.

Perhaps she simply needed someone to come close enough to say:

You are allowed to belong to yourself, too.

Knowing Is Not the Same as Being Able to Leave

There is a temptation, when we look backward, to make healing seem obvious.

We find the warning signs.

We identify the moments our stomach tightened or our breath changed.

We remember the friend who asked the question.

The stranger who said the thing aloud.

And from the safety of the future, we wonder how we could possibly have missed what now seems so clear.

But perhaps Why didn’t I listen? is not always the most useful question.

Sometimes we did listen.

Sometimes some part of us heard everything.

The harder truth is that knowing something and having the capacity to reorganize our lives around that knowing are not the same thing.

Our bodies can register fear while another part of us is desperately protecting attachment.

Hope can become a survival strategy.

So can minimizing.

So can performing happiness.

So can believing that this time will finally be different.

The body gives us information, but that does not mean every sensation is a prophecy or every fear is an instruction.

Embodiment asks something more compassionate of us than simply “trust your gut.”

It asks us to become curious about the whole conversation happening inside.

What is my body noticing?

What is my fear protecting?

What relationship am I afraid to lose?

What would listening to myself require?

And do I have enough safety and support to bear what I might hear?

The younger version of me did not need more shame for ignoring her body.

She needed enough safety to remain in relationship with herself when the truth became difficult to hold.

That would come later.

Perhaps reclamation often does.

Not in the instant we first know.

But in the moment we finally have enough support, language, agency, and compassion to stop abandoning ourselves for knowing.

With Eternal Gratitude,
Alynne

This week’s practice

As you continue your journey of remembering, returning, and reclaiming, I leave you with this week’s practice.

Remember

You may have known something before you could name it.

You may even have heard the warning clearly and still been unable to reorganize your life around what it meant.

The knowing was not meaningless simply because you could not act on it yet.

Sometimes remembering begins when we stop asking the earlier version of ourselves why she failed to act and become curious about what she was trying desperately to preserve.

Return

Place a hand somewhere on your body where you tend to notice hesitation, discomfort, or uncertainty.

You do not have to decide what the sensation means.

You do not have to turn it into an answer.

Simply notice.

“What am I aware of right now?”

Then, if it feels possible, ask:

“What happens inside me when I imagine honoring what I notice?”

Perhaps a sensation comes.

An image.

An emotion.

An impulse.

A memory.

Perhaps nothing comes at all.

There is no correct response.

For a few breaths, simply practice staying in conversation with yourself.

Reclaim

This week, notice one moment when your body gives you information before your mind has formed an explanation.

Instead of immediately overriding the sensation, or immediately obeying it, try becoming curious.

“What might this be asking me to notice?”

And perhaps the more important question:

“What would help me feel safe enough to stay in conversation with myself here?”

Reclamation does not require perfect intuition.

Sometimes it begins much more quietly.

By becoming someone whose own experience is finally allowed a place at the table.
