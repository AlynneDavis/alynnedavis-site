Letters from the Studio, Letter One

What Is an Embodied Reclamation?

The story behind a philosophy of healing rooted in remembering, returning, and reclaiming. The first of many letters inviting you on the journey back to yourself.

When Knowing Isn’t Enough

Have you ever known exactly what to do… yet still felt disconnected from yourself?

If so, you’re not alone.

For years, I believed healing began with changing our thoughts.

As both a therapist and a fellow human being, I’ve come to believe something different.

Understanding ourselves is important, but it isn’t always enough.

Sometimes the missing piece isn’t more insight.

It’s learning how to return to ourselves.

The Search for Something More

For as long as I can remember, I’ve been curious.

Curious about people.

Curious about creativity.

Curious about why some experiences shape us so deeply while others seem to pass right through us.

Looking back now, I can see that my healing journey didn’t begin when I became a therapist. It began long before I had the language to describe it. It unfolded through childhood experiences, relationships, heartbreak, creativity, and countless moments of searching for a deeper understanding of myself.

I didn’t know it then, but I was already beginning the work of reclamation.

Years later, after spending seventeen years in a career that I genuinely loved, I felt an undeniable pull toward something deeper.

I had built a successful life in the beauty industry, but there was a quiet voice inside me that kept whispering there was another way I was meant to serve.

That whisper eventually led me to graduate school, where I trained as both a clinical mental health counselor and an expressive arts therapist.

I thought I was going there to learn how to help other people heal.

Instead, it became one of the greatest healing experiences of my own life.

I had been to therapy before.

Talk therapy helped me understand myself.

It gave me language for my experiences.

It helped me recognize patterns.

It helped me make sense of my life.

But if I’m honest…

I didn’t feel healed.

I understood myself better.

But I still felt disconnected from myself.

That distinction changed everything.

Graduate school invited me into an entirely different way of knowing.

Through expressive arts, movement, music, mindfulness, creativity, and deep reflection, I began experiencing healing instead of simply thinking about it.

The Day Everything Changed

I remember the day everything broke open.

It was the day I finally understood why my professors kept encouraging us to “trust the process.”

If I’m honest, going back to graduate school in my thirties felt both exciting and terrifying. The way we were invited to learn was unlike anything I had ever experienced. We weren’t just studying theories, we were living them. Much of what we were practicing felt completely foreign to me, yet something deep inside kept whispering to stay curious and keep going.

One afternoon, during a three-hour expressive arts class, we spent the entire time deepening the process through art making, music, movement, and finally, clay.

I can still remember the earthy smell of the moist clay on my hands.

In front of me sat the figures I had sculpted of my family.

My two children leaned into my clay body, seeking shelter and protection.

Behind me stood the clay figure of their father, larger, looming, my back turned toward him.

The moment I looked at what my hands had created, something inside me shattered.

I burst into deep, guttural sobs that seemed to come from somewhere far beneath words.

I felt the grief.

I felt the fear.

I felt the unbearable weight of years spent believing it was my responsibility to protect everyone else, even from the one person I was supposed to feel safest with.

As I curled into a fetal position on the floor, held by the quiet presence and compassion of my classmates, something profound shifted.

No one analyzed me.

No one tried to fix me.

No one told me what the sculpture meant.

My body had simply told the truth before my mind was ready to speak it.

That was the moment I began to remember who I was.

The Question That Changed Everything

I didn’t know it then, but that day became the beginning of a question that has shaped the last fifteen years of my life.

Why did I feel more connected to myself after one afternoon of creating with clay than after years of simply understanding my story?

Why had my body revealed something my mind had struggled to name?

Those questions stayed with me.

As I continued my own healing and began walking alongside clients in theirs, I noticed a pattern. So many people were incredibly insightful. They knew their attachment patterns. They understood their trauma. They could explain why they struggled with anxiety, perfectionism, disordered eating, or relationships.

And yet…

Many still didn’t feel free.

The more I witnessed this, in my own life and in the lives of the people I served, the more I realized that awareness is essential, but awareness alone doesn’t always create transformation.

Healing asks something more of us.

It asks us to reconnect with the parts of ourselves we’ve learned to ignore, protect, silence, or abandon.

It asks us to return to the wisdom of the body, where so much of our story has been held all along.

That realization slowly became the foundation of what I now call Embodied Reclamation.

What Is an Embodied Reclamation?

Healing, I’ve come to believe, is remembering rather than fixing.

We are not broken objects waiting to be repaired.

We are human beings whose bodies have carried stories all along, stories of survival, love, loss, resilience, shame, creativity, and hope.

Many of the strategies that keep us feeling stuck were once brilliant ways of helping us survive.

Our work is not to wage war against those parts.

It is to meet them with curiosity and compassion while gently remembering the authentic self that has been present all along.

To me, Embodied Reclamation is the lifelong practice of coming home to yourself.

It is remembering who you were before shame convinced you otherwise.

It is returning to your body through curiosity, creativity, and compassionate awareness.

And it is reclaiming your voice, your authenticity, your relationships, your joy, and your place in the world, not by becoming someone new, but by courageously embodying the person you’ve always been.

A Letter to You

If you’ve found your way here, perhaps part of you is already longing to return home to yourself.

My hope is that these letters become a place where you feel seen, challenged, inspired, and gently invited back to the parts of yourself that have always been there.

I’m grateful you’re here.

Welcome home.

With Eternal Gratitude,
Alynne

This week’s practice

Remember

Healing isn’t about becoming someone new.

It’s about remembering the person you’ve always been beneath the layers of protection, performance, and pain.

Return

Take five quiet minutes this week.

Place one hand on your heart and one on your belly.

Instead of asking yourself, What’s wrong with me?

Ask:

“What part of me is asking to be remembered?”

Reclaim

Notice one place this week where you’re living from a should.

Then gently ask yourself:

“Is this truly mine, or is it a story I inherited?”
