When the Food Noise Quiets: What I See in Women on GLP-1 Medications

The medication is often not the thing causing the harm. The undereating underneath it is, and for many women that started long before the prescription did.

By Alynne Davis, MA, LCMHC  •  August 30, 2026

The food noise stopped.

That constant background negotiation about what you ate and what you will eat and what it means, quiet for the first time in years. If you have spent two decades with that voice running, the silence can feel like being handed your life back.

That part is real. I am not here to talk you out of it.

What nobody warned you about is what comes after.

The cascade

You cannot concentrate. Your focus has scattered in a way it never has before, and you have started to wonder whether you have ADHD.

You are not sleeping well. Your hair is thinning. Your skin is dry. Your digestion has slowed, because there is not enough going in to keep it moving. You have no interest in sex and cannot pinpoint when that changed. Your strength is going, particularly if you are not lifting or eating enough protein to hold onto muscle.

And underneath all of it, you have no hunger signal left at all. So you forget to eat entirely, which makes every one of those things worse.

I see the same pattern in men, and age sharpens it for everyone. When collagen and skin elasticity are already declining, undereating on top of that shows up faster and takes longer to come back from.

Here is what I have come to think. The medication is not doing most of that. The undereating is.

The medication quieted the signal that would have told you that you were undereating. Everything after that followed from a body that stopped being fed.

For many of you, the undereating started long before the medication

This is the part I want to be careful to say clearly, because it is what I spend most of my time on with women who binge.

A great many women arrive already undereating. Not dramatically. Not in a way anyone would call a disorder. Coffee instead of breakfast. A small lunch, eaten standing up, if at all. Being “good” all day.

And then something gives way in the evening, and they eat in a way that frightens them, and they wake up ashamed and resolve to be stricter tomorrow.

What almost nobody tells them is that the strictness is causing the thing they are ashamed of. A body that has been underfed all day is not making a moral choice at nine at night. It is collecting what it is owed. The bingeing is not a discipline failure. It is the predictable second half of the restriction.

Here is the part that is hard to hear. That pattern can also be part of why the weight is not shifting. A body that keeps being underfed learns to hold on. So the harder she tries, the more stuck she becomes, and the more certain she is that she simply lacks willpower.

So when a woman like this starts a GLP-1, she is not correcting the problem. She is adding to it. The medication removes what little appetite signal she had left, on top of an eating pattern that was already too restricted, and the cycle she was hoping to escape gets quieter and deeper at the same time.

For some women the noise stopping is genuinely the first peace they have had. For others it means the last warning light just went out.

Three things wearing the same face

This is where it gets genuinely difficult.

You are in your forties. You cannot focus, you cannot hold a thought, your memory has gone unreliable, and you are exhausted in a way sleep does not touch. You are fairly sure it is ADHD.

It might be. Late diagnosis in women is real and it is common.

It might also be perimenopause, where those exact symptoms are well documented and routinely missed.

Or you might be undereating badly on a medication that removed your ability to notice.

Or, and this is what I see most, some combination of all three at once, each one making the others harder to see. Perimenopause is already disrupting your hormones. The undereating compounds it. And what arrives in my office looks exactly like ADHD.

I cannot tell you from here which it is. What I can tell you is that the answer matters enormously, and that almost nobody is asking the question in that order.

The risk that has nothing to do with the medication

There is a second thing I watch for, and it worries me more than the physical side.

If you come to a GLP-1 with a body image disorder that was never treated, or body dysmorphia, or trauma you have not processed, the weight loss does not resolve any of it. It reactivates it. Sometimes it entrenches it further, because now there is a prescription behind the restriction and nobody questions it.

The thing I see most often is this. You believe that if you can just lose the weight, you will finally like yourself. That your relationship with yourself will settle, and your relationships with everyone else will follow.

It does not work that way. I have never once seen it work that way.

The body was the symptom. Underneath it is almost always something older: insecurity that started long before this body did, and a sense of worth that got attached to appearance at an age when you had no say in it. Losing weight does not touch that. It removes the thing you were blaming and leaves you standing in front of the actual problem with fewer places to look.

That moment is often when women come to me. Not when they are struggling to lose weight. When they have lost it and nothing they were promised has arrived.

I have felt the pull myself

I want to be honest with you, because I am not writing this from above it.

When perimenopause really hit, I noticed my midsection had become much harder to maintain, and that the only thing reliably offering me comfort was sugar and fried food. I am a single mother in my mid-forties, raising teenagers on my own. I would like to age well and to feel good in my body while I do it. That is not vanity. That is being a person.

It landed hardest at a point when I was already stretched thin, which is generally when these things land.

I went to a functional medicine practice for a treatment that promised inches lost in a session, and the doctor there recommended tirzepatide.

And my body tensed before my mind had caught up. I felt defensive, almost braced, sitting in that chair. I had not decided anything yet and something in me had already answered.

I want to be honest about the rest of it too, because this is the part women do not say out loud. There was a spark. A small hit of something that loves a shortcut. For a moment I let myself imagine having the body I had at twenty-two, and wearing whatever I wanted.

And then I thought: that was not when you were happy.

Being thin never once helped my mental health. If anything it made it worse. So I came out of the daydream and said no thank you, and chose to go a slower way.

But I understand it now in a way I did not before. Especially for women in larger bodies, who have been judged and corrected and commented on their whole lives. There is something almost Hollywood about the promise. It feels like it could lift off all the shame that has been put on women simply for being here in a body.

I do not think less of anyone who feels that pull. I felt it in a doctor’s office at forty-something, with fifteen years of this work behind me.

Nobody is screening for any of this

Most prescribers are not asking about:

  • Past or current eating disorder history
  • Trauma history
  • How food has functioned as coping
  • Whether hunger cues were ever reliable to begin with
  • Bone density and muscle mass, particularly in women already at risk

The National Eating Disorders Association has warned that these medications can reactivate restrictive patterns and silence hunger cues in people with eating disorder histories, including years into recovery. ANAD has raised similar concerns.

None of that makes these medications wrong for everyone. It means that if you have that history, you deserve to be asked before you start, and very often you will not be.

So what do I actually say?

When a woman tells me she is considering one, I do not tell her not to. I ask about motive.

Is there a genuine medical reason? Diabetes, metabolic conditions, or in some cases interrupting a binge and reward cycle that has not responded to anything else. Those are real, and I have seen these medications help.

Where are you right now with food, nutrition, and your body? Someone who has done no work on any of it is in a very different position from someone who has.

And the question underneath both: are you looking for a quick fix, or a tool?

A GLP-1 is not a treatment on its own. It works alongside changes you still have to make, and if you are hoping it will do the work instead of you, it will not, and the fall afterward is harder than never starting.

Then I ask the one that tells me the most.

Are you willing to be honest with your providers, and to let them talk to each other?

I would go as far as a behavioural agreement written into the treatment plan, revisited as you go, with your therapist, prescriber, and dietitian actually coordinating rather than each holding one piece.

Whether you are open to that is, in itself, the answer. A woman who wants support around this says yes immediately. A woman who wants the medication and not the accountability hesitates, and that hesitation is worth both of us paying attention to.

These medications are not going away, and neither are the peptides coming after them. The question was never whether women will take them. It is whether anyone is helping you work out if it is the right fit, and staying with you once it starts.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can GLP-1 medications trigger an eating disorder?

For someone with a history of restriction, bingeing, or body image distress, they can reactivate patterns that were previously in remission, sometimes years into recovery. The National Eating Disorders Association has raised this specifically. The appetite suppression that makes these medications effective also removes the hunger cues that eating disorder recovery depends on rebuilding, which can make undereating feel effortless and go unnoticed.

Why can I not focus since starting a GLP-1?

Difficulty concentrating, unreliable memory, and exhaustion that sleep does not resolve are common when the body is significantly underfed. In women in their forties this becomes harder to untangle, because undereating, perimenopause, and ADHD can produce very similar symptoms, and often more than one is present at once. It is worth being assessed for all three rather than assuming which it is.

Will losing weight improve my body image?

Usually not on its own. Body image distress is rarely about the body itself. It is more often rooted in self-worth, earlier experiences, and beliefs formed long before the current body existed. Many women lose weight and find their body image is unchanged or worse, because the underlying issue was never addressed. That is often the point at which they seek therapy.

Should I stop taking my GLP-1 if I recognise these patterns?

That is a decision for you and your prescriber, not something to decide from an article. What matters more is that you stop doing it without support. That generally means therapy addressing what food has been holding, a dietitian who specialises in eating disorders, medical monitoring that looks beyond weight, and enough protein and strength work to protect muscle and bone.

Is undereating why I keep bingeing at night?

Very often, yes. A body underfed through the day is not making a moral choice in the evening, it is collecting what it is owed. The bingeing is usually the second half of the restriction rather than a separate problem, which is why eating more consistently during the day tends to reduce it more effectively than trying harder to resist.
