What We Are Still Carrying: The Difference Between Letting Go and Learning to Release

On what happens when we are no longer holding on to a person or an outcome, but our bodies are still living with the consequences of what occurred.

By Alynne Davis, MA, LCMHC  •  November 23, 2026

There is a particular kind of fatigue that can arrive after the emotional work has been done. We have named what happened. We have stopped hoping the other person will finally understand, apologize, or change. A message from them no longer sends us into the old cycle of explaining and defending, and we recognize, sometimes with real surprise, that we have changed. And yet the body has not quite caught up. Sleep is still light. Digestion is unpredictable. A tightness gathers under the ribs when a legal document arrives. It can feel as though we should be further along by now.

I have been sitting with this in my own life. After years of prolonged relational stress, I can feel how differently I now respond to someone around whom my nervous system was once organized. I hold boundaries I could not hold before, and I no longer take responsibility for choices that were never mine. Something in me has let go. At the same time, I have become more curious about my body: about my digestion, about the hormonal shifts of perimenopause, and about what years of sustained vigilance might ask of a body even after the heart has moved on. That curiosity is where this essay began.

Holding on, and holding everything together

The language of healing often treats letting go as the finish line. If we are still affected, the reasoning goes, we must not have released it yet. But there is an important difference between holding on emotionally and having to hold everything together. Holding on is attachment to a person, an outcome, or the hope that what happened will eventually be made right. Holding everything together is labor. It is the logistics of a life reorganized by someone else’s choices: the financial recalculations, the co-parenting exchanges, and the years of rebuilding stability while keeping a household, a career, and often children steady. We can release the first and still be deep inside the second. When the consequences of an experience are ongoing, the body is not only responding to a memory. It is responding to a present that continues to require something of it.

The things we were never given time to digest

I recently came across the phrase “indigestible morsel” in German New Medicine, a framework claiming that specific emotional conflicts produce specific illnesses. Those medical claims are not supported by scientific evidence, and I do not endorse them. Illness is not a verdict on our emotional lives. Still, the image stayed with me, because it names an experience many women know: being handed something we cannot take in, whether a betrayal that contradicts everything we believed about a person, an injustice the systems meant to address it simply absorb, or a loss we did not choose and cannot reverse.

Digestion works as a metaphor precisely because it is a process rather than an event. It involves receiving something, breaking it into parts we can work with, integrating what nourishes us, and eliminating what we cannot use. The difficulty with chronic relational stress is not that we fail at this process but that we are rarely given the time it requires. When we are asked to digest new injuries before we have had the chance to process the previous ones, the backlog is not a personal failing. It is a reasonable response to an unreasonable pace.

What the body is actually doing

The metaphor deserves to be kept separate from what we know physiologically. The gut and the brain are in constant, bidirectional communication through the vagus nerve, the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis that governs the stress hormone response, the immune system, and the microbes that live in the digestive tract, a network researchers describe as the microbiota-gut-brain axis. When the autonomic nervous system perceives threat, it shifts resources toward readiness and away from rest and digestion. Over months and years, sustained activation of the stress response is associated with changes in gut motility, sensitivity, and barrier function, along with disrupted sleep and inflammation. The neuroendocrinologist Bruce McEwen called this cumulative cost allostatic load: the wear that builds when a body is asked to adapt continuously without adequate recovery.

A body that remains on alert while legal, financial, or co-parenting stressors continue is not malfunctioning. It is doing what bodies do when circumstances keep signaling that vigilance is needed.

For many women, this season also overlaps with perimenopause. Fluctuating estrogen and progesterone influence sleep, mood, and the stress response, and estrogen has documented effects on gut motility and the composition of the gut microbiome. Changes in digestion, sleep, and anxiety during these years are common and frequently dismissed, when several systems are often shifting at once. They deserve evaluation by a provider who takes them seriously, not an assumption that they are purely emotional or purely hormonal.

For women with a history of disordered eating, this territory is especially familiar. Restriction, bingeing, and purging are themselves physiological stressors, and digestive symptoms such as bloating, early fullness, constipation, and reflux are common both during an eating disorder and well into recovery. Many women find they have let go of the behaviors, and much of the fear around food, while their digestion is still relearning how to trust regular nourishment. That lag is the same distinction this essay is about. It is also why digestive discomfort in recovery deserves patience and medical support rather than being read as a sign of relapse, and why new digestive changes in midlife can quietly draw old patterns of scrutiny and control back toward the surface.

What we were taught to swallow

The metaphor of digestion resonates with women for another reason. Many of us were conditioned early to swallow our anger, to tolerate discomfort without complaint, to keep the peace, and to make our needs small enough not to inconvenience anyone. We learned to absorb rather than respond. Those were intelligent adaptations in environments where speaking up was costly. But years of swallowing what we could not say leave us skilled at endurance and far less practiced at noticing what endurance costs. In my work as a therapist, I often see this pattern reach a woman’s relationship with food and her body, where hunger, fullness, and fatigue become one more set of signals she overrides in order to keep functioning.

Acceptance, forgiveness, reconciliation, and release

It helps to separate several words often used interchangeably. Acceptance is acknowledging what happened, and what is still happening, without requiring it to be otherwise. Forgiveness, if it comes, is an internal release of the demand that another person repay what they took. It is a choice rather than an obligation, and it does not require their participation. Reconciliation is relational and depends on safety and accountability from both people, which is why it is sometimes neither possible nor wise. Release is quieter: loosening our grip on the hope that the other person will change or that the story will resolve as it should have.

We can accept without reconciling, release without forgiving, and do all of these while still grieving. That grief is not only for what happened. It is also for what it cost us: the years, the money, the family we had planned, and the health we spent holding it all together. Grieving the cost is not staying attached to the cause.

Healing inside an unfinished situation

Within Embodied Reclamation, this is the territory the RRR Method™ holds, though not as a sequence of steps. To remember is to recognize the experiences, conditioning, and internal agreements that taught us to tolerate more than was ours to carry, agreements such as I will be the one who copes or I will not make this harder than it already is. To return is to come back to the body with curiosity, noticing hunger, fatigue, where tension gathers, and when something in us finally settles. Return runs through the whole process, because some of what we carry cannot be understood through thought alone. To reclaim is to let that awareness shape how we live now: the boundaries we hold, the responsibilities we decline, the rest we protect, and the care we offer ourselves even though we cannot change the past or control another person’s behavior.

Running through all three is compassionate honesty, the practice of telling ourselves the truth without abandoning ourselves in the process. Here it means acknowledging the full weight of what we have lived through without minimizing it, without blaming ourselves for its effects, and without expecting our bodies to recover on an arbitrary timeline.

Healing does not require that the external situation be finished. Many women wait for the final court date, the last payment, or the other person’s acknowledgment before allowing themselves to begin restoring. Restoration can happen alongside ongoing stress. It may look like eating regular, nourishing meals even when appetite is unreliable, protecting sleep, moving gently, having physical symptoms evaluated rather than dismissed, and spending time in relationships and places where vigilance is not required. None of these erase the stressor. They give the nervous system repeated, concrete experiences of safety, and those accumulate too.

A body that does not have to prove anything

Physical symptoms are not evidence that we have failed to heal emotionally. Wellness culture makes it tempting to read a flare of digestive trouble or a sleepless week as proof that we are still holding something. That turns the body into a report card for our emotional progress. Bodies carry their own histories, genetics, hormones, and timelines, and they deserve care whether or not they ever become symbols of recovery.

So rather than a solution, I would like to leave you with a question. What would it mean to care for your body without needing it to prove anything? To feed it, rest it, and listen to it because it has carried you through something difficult, not because its symptoms must disappear before you can call yourself healed? If you are no longer holding on but are still holding everything together, you might begin simply by noticing what your body is doing, and meeting it with the patience you would offer anyone who had lived through what you have. That noticing is not the end of the work. It may be where the next part of it begins.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I have let go of something emotionally and still feel it in my body?

Yes. Releasing your attachment to a person or an outcome is different from recovering from the ongoing consequences of what happened. When financial, legal, or co-parenting stress continues, the nervous system is responding to a present that still asks something of it, not only to a memory. Physical stress responses can continue long after the emotional attachment has eased.

How does chronic stress affect digestion?

The gut and brain communicate in both directions through the vagus nerve, the HPA (stress hormone) axis, the immune system, and the gut microbiome. When the body perceives ongoing threat, it shifts resources away from rest and digestion. Over time, sustained stress is associated with changes in gut motility, sensitivity, and barrier function, along with disrupted sleep and inflammation. Digestive symptoms should still be evaluated by a medical provider rather than assumed to be stress alone.

Can perimenopause affect digestion and how I handle stress?

It can. Fluctuating estrogen and progesterone influence sleep, mood, and the stress response, and estrogen affects gut motility and the gut microbiome. Many women in perimenopause notice changes in digestion, bloating, sleep, and anxiety at the same time. These symptoms are often the product of several systems shifting at once and deserve evaluation from a provider who takes them seriously.

What is the difference between acceptance, forgiveness, reconciliation, and release?

Acceptance is acknowledging what happened and what is still happening. Forgiveness is an internal release of the demand that another person repay what they took, and it is a choice rather than an obligation. Reconciliation is relational and depends on safety and accountability from both people, so it is sometimes neither possible nor wise. Release is loosening your grip on the hope that the other person will change or that the story will resolve the way it should have. You can accept without reconciling and release without forgiving.

Do physical symptoms mean I have not healed emotionally?

No. Physical symptoms are not evidence of emotional failure, and illness is not caused by unresolved feelings. Bodies carry their own histories, genetics, hormones, and timelines. Treating every symptom as proof that you are still holding something turns the body into a report card. Your body deserves care whether or not it ever becomes a symbol of your recovery.
