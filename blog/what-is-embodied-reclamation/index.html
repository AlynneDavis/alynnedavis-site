What Is Embodied Reclamation? An Expressive Arts Therapist’s Story of Coming Home to Yourself

Have you ever known exactly what to do… yet still felt disconnected from yourself? If so, you are not alone. This is the story behind a philosophy of healing rooted in remembering, returning, and reclaiming, and an invitation back to the parts of you that have been there all along.

By Alynne Davis, MA, LCMHC  •  July 22, 2026

For years, I believed healing began with changing our thoughts. As both a therapist and a fellow human being, I have come to believe something different: understanding ourselves is important, but it isn’t always enough. Sometimes the missing piece isn’t more insight… it is learning how to return to ourselves.

That single shift, from understanding myself to returning to myself, became the foundation of everything I now call Embodied Reclamation. Here is how I found my way to it.

The Search for Something More

For as long as I can remember, I have been curious. Curious about people. Curious about creativity. Curious about why some experiences shape us so deeply while others seem to pass right through us.

Looking back now, I can see that my healing journey didn’t begin when I became a therapist. It began long before I had the language to describe it, through childhood experiences, relationships, heartbreak, creativity, and countless moments of searching for a deeper understanding of myself. I didn’t know it then, but I was already beginning the work of reclamation.

Years later, after seventeen years in a career I genuinely loved, I felt an undeniable pull toward something deeper. I had built a successful life in the beauty industry, but there was a quiet voice inside me that kept whispering there was another way I was meant to serve. That whisper eventually led me to graduate school, where I trained as both a clinical mental health counselor and an expressive arts therapist.

I thought I was going there to learn how to help other people heal. Instead, it became one of the greatest healing experiences of my own life.

I had been to therapy before. Talk therapy helped me understand myself. It gave me language for my experiences. It helped me recognize patterns and make sense of my life. But if I am honest… I didn’t feel healed. I understood myself better, and I still felt disconnected from myself.

That distinction changed everything. Graduate school invited me into an entirely different way of knowing. Through expressive arts, movement, music, mindfulness, creativity, and deep reflection, I began to experience healing rather than simply thinking about it.

The Day Everything Changed

I remember the day everything broke open. It was the day I finally understood why my professors kept encouraging us to “trust the process.”

If I am honest, going back to graduate school in my thirties felt both exciting and terrifying. The way we were invited to learn was unlike anything I had ever experienced. We weren’t just studying theories… we were living them. Much of what we practiced felt completely foreign to me, yet something deep inside kept whispering to stay curious and keep going.

One afternoon, during a three-hour expressive arts class, we spent the entire time deepening the process through art making, music, movement, and finally, clay. I can still remember the earthy smell of the moist clay on my hands.

In front of me sat the figures I had sculpted of my family. My two children leaned into my clay body, seeking shelter and protection. Behind me stood the clay figure of their father, larger, looming, my back turned toward him.

The moment I looked at what my hands had created, something inside me shattered. I burst into deep, guttural sobs that seemed to come from somewhere far beneath words. I felt the grief. I felt the fear. I felt the unbearable weight of years spent believing it was my responsibility to protect everyone else… even from the one person I was supposed to feel safest with.

As I curled into a fetal position on the floor, held by the quiet presence and compassion of my classmates, something profound shifted. No one analyzed me. No one tried to fix me. No one told me what the sculpture meant. My body had simply told the truth before my mind was ready to speak it.

That was the moment I began to remember who I was.

The Question That Changed Everything

I didn’t know it then, but that day became the beginning of a question that has shaped the last fifteen years of my life. Why did I feel more connected to myself after one afternoon of creating with clay than after years of simply understanding my story? Why had my body revealed something my mind had struggled to name?

Those questions stayed with me. As I continued my own healing and began walking alongside clients in theirs, I noticed a pattern. So many people were incredibly insightful. They knew their attachment patterns. They understood their trauma. They could explain why they struggled with anxiety, perfectionism, disordered eating, or relationships.

And yet… many still didn’t feel free.

The more I witnessed this, in my own life and in the lives of the people I served, the more I realized that awareness is essential, but awareness alone doesn’t always create transformation. Healing asks something more of us. It asks us to reconnect with the parts of ourselves we have learned to ignore, protect, silence, or abandon. It asks us to return to the wisdom of the body, where so much of our story has been held all along.

That realization slowly became the foundation of what I now call Embodied Reclamation.

What Is Embodied Reclamation?

Healing, I have come to believe, is remembering rather than fixing. We are not broken objects waiting to be repaired. We are human beings whose bodies have carried stories all along, stories of survival, love, loss, resilience, shame, creativity, and hope.

Many of the strategies that keep us feeling stuck were once brilliant ways of helping us survive. Our work is not to wage war against those parts. It is to meet them with curiosity and compassion while gently remembering the authentic self that has been present all along.

To me, Embodied Reclamation is the lifelong practice of coming home to yourself. It is remembering who you were before shame convinced you otherwise. It is returning to your body through curiosity, creativity, and compassionate awareness. And it is reclaiming your voice, your authenticity, your relationships, your joy, and your place in the world, not by becoming someone new, but by courageously embodying the person you have always been.

This is the heart of the RRR Method™ at the center of my practice:

  • Remember, your inherent worth, your authentic identity, and what has always been true beneath survival, performance, and pain.
  • Return, to your body, your emotions, your creativity, your relationships, and self-compassion.
  • Reclaim, your voice, your agency, your boundaries, your joy, and your freedom.

How Expressive Arts Therapy Helps You Return to Yourself

If my own story taught me anything, it is this: for many people, insight is not the missing ingredient. Connection is. This is exactly where expressive arts therapy lives.

Expressive arts therapy integrates creative practices, drawing, writing, movement, music, and reflection, to help you access emotions and insights that are difficult to reach through words alone. You do not need to be artistic. It is not about making something beautiful or skillful. It is about exploration, and about letting the body contribute to the conversation your mind has been carrying by itself.

This kind of body-based, creative work is especially supportive for women who feel intellectually aware but emotionally stuck, women healing from trauma, navigating perfectionism and people-pleasing, or working to rebuild a compassionate relationship with their bodies and with food. When talking about what happened has not been enough, the creative process can gently open a different door.

To read more about how body-centered approaches work alongside this, you may find What Is Somatic Therapy? a helpful companion piece.

A Gentle Practice to Try This Week

You do not have to wait for a therapy session to begin remembering, returning, and reclaiming. Here is a small practice, drawn from the three movements of the RRR Method™.

Remember. Healing isn’t about becoming someone new. It is about remembering the person you have always been beneath the layers of protection, performance, and pain.

Return. Take five quiet minutes this week. Place one hand on your heart and one on your belly. Instead of asking yourself, “What’s wrong with me?” ask, “What part of me is asking to be remembered?”

Reclaim. Notice one place this week where you are living from a “should.” Then gently ask yourself: “Is this truly mine… or is it a story I inherited?”

If part of you is already longing to return home to yourself, please know that longing is not a problem to solve. It is a doorway.

Frequently Asked Questions About Embodied Reclamation

What is Embodied Reclamation?

Embodied Reclamation is a philosophy of healing built on the belief that we are not broken and do not need to be fixed. It is the lifelong practice of coming home to yourself, remembering who you were before shame convinced you otherwise, returning to your body through curiosity, creativity, and compassionate awareness, and reclaiming your voice, authenticity, relationships, and joy. It treats the body not as a problem to manage but as a source of wisdom that has carried your story all along.

What is the RRR Method™ (Remember, Return, Reclaim)?

The RRR Method is the framework at the heart of Embodied Reclamation. Remember means remembering your inherent worth and authentic identity beneath survival, performance, and pain. Return means returning to your body, emotional awareness, creativity, and self-compassion. Reclaim means reclaiming your voice, agency, boundaries, and joy, not by becoming someone new, but by embodying the person you have always been.

How is expressive arts therapy different from talk therapy?

Talk therapy helps you understand yourself, it gives language to your experiences and helps you recognize patterns. Expressive arts therapy goes further by inviting the body and creativity into the healing process through art making, movement, music, and reflection. Many people find they can understand their story intellectually and still feel disconnected from themselves. Expressive arts therapy helps you experience healing rather than only thinking about it, reaching places that words alone often cannot.

Do I need to be artistic to benefit from expressive arts therapy?

Not at all. Expressive arts therapy is not about artistic skill or making something beautiful. It is about exploration, curiosity, and connecting with your inner experience in new ways. There is no right way to do it, and no experience is required. The goal is not a finished product, it is what the process reveals and releases.
